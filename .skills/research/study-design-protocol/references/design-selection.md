# Design Selection

Choose a design from the inference required, not from convenience alone.

| Question | Candidate design | Main caution |
|---|---|---|
| Does an assigned intervention change an outcome? | Randomized experiment | Allocation, adherence, contamination, and external validity |
| How does an exposure relate to later outcomes? | Prospective cohort | Confounding, loss to follow-up, exposure measurement |
| What is the frequency or distribution at a time point? | Cross-sectional study | Temporality and prevalence/incidence distinction |
| What does a policy or event change? | Quasi-experiment | Identification assumptions and concurrent changes |
| How do people experience or interpret a phenomenon? | Qualitative design | Sampling rationale, context, reflexivity, analytic transparency |
| How do estimates and experiences complement each other? | Mixed methods | Integration must be planned, not appended |

## Define the target inference

Write what the study will estimate or understand. Quantitative protocols should state the target population, treatment or exposure condition, comparator, outcome, follow-up, and estimand when applicable. Qualitative protocols should state the phenomenon, context, participant perspective, and analytic purpose.

## Design decisions

- Select the unit of assignment, observation, and analysis; explain mismatches.
- Choose timing that supports temporal ordering where needed.
- Define comparison conditions and practical alternatives.
- Identify likely selection, information, confounding, and measurement biases.
- State assumptions required for interpretation and how they will be examined.
- For mixed methods, specify sequence, priority, and integration points.

No design label alone guarantees validity. Match procedures and analysis to the actual question and constraints.
