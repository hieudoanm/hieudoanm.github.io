# Research Gap

## Purpose

Use this skill to identify and validate a genuine gap in the research literature and translate that gap into a focused research question.

A research gap is not simply something a paper says should be studied next. A defensible gap is an important uncertainty, limitation, inconsistency, missing evidence, or unexplored relationship that remains after examining the relevant literature.

The goal is not to find the most novel-sounding gap.

The goal is to find the **most defensible important gap supported by the current evidence**.

## Core principle

> Do not search for a gap sentence. Map the evidence, identify what remains unresolved, and verify that the gap still exists.

Use this reasoning process:

```text
Research topic
      ↓
Define scope
      ↓
Map the literature
      ↓
Establish what is known
      ↓
Identify uncertainty and disagreement
      ↓
Identify limitations and missing evidence
      ↓
Generate candidate gaps
      ↓
Check recent literature
      ↓
Validate the gap
      ↓
Assess importance and feasibility
      ↓
Formulate the gap
      ↓
Generate research question
```

## What counts as a research gap?

A research gap may involve:

- **Knowledge** — an important phenomenon remains poorly understood.
- **Evidence** — insufficient empirical evidence exists.
- **Methodology** — existing methods cannot adequately answer an important question.
- **Population** — an important population is under-studied.
- **Data** — important data are missing, inaccessible, sparse, or insufficient.
- **Theory** — existing theories do not adequately explain the evidence.
- **Replication** — an important finding has limited independent verification.
- **Integration** — related literatures have not been meaningfully connected.
- **Application** — a finding or method has not been adequately tested in an important practical context.

These categories can overlap.

Do not force every gap into exactly one category.

## Workflow

### 1. Define the research scope

Before searching for gaps, define the boundaries of the problem.

Identify, where relevant:

- Research topic
- Research phenomenon
- Population
- Context
- Intervention or exposure
- Comparison
- Outcome
- Methods
- Data type
- Relevant disciplines
- Time period

A broad topic such as:

> "Speech and the brain"

is usually too broad.

A more useful scope might be:

> Neural decoding of speech and semantic representations from non-invasive neuroimaging in healthy adults.

The scope should be narrow enough to support meaningful comparison across studies.

---

### 2. Build a literature map

Do not read papers as isolated summaries.

For important studies, extract:

- Research question
- Hypothesis
- Population/sample
- Dataset
- Experimental design
- Measurements
- Preprocessing
- Analysis methods
- Main findings
- Limitations
- Claims supported by evidence
- Claims that remain uncertain
- Relationship to previous studies

The purpose is to understand the **structure of the evidence base**.

Look for:

```text
What has been studied?
        ↓
How has it been studied?
        ↓
What has been found?
        ↓
Where do findings agree?
        ↓
Where do findings disagree?
        ↓
What remains uncertain?
```

---

### 3. Establish what is known

Separate evidence into levels of confidence.

Useful categories include:

- Well established
- Supported by multiple studies
- Preliminary
- Mixed or inconsistent
- Unresolved
- Unsupported

Do not describe an area as a "gap" simply because one study produced an unexpected result.

First determine what the broader literature shows.

---

### 4. Identify candidate gaps

Search systematically for patterns such as:

#### Missing knowledge

An important mechanism, relationship, or phenomenon has not been adequately explained.

#### Conflicting evidence

Different studies produce inconsistent results that cannot be explained by an obvious methodological difference.

#### Methodological limitations

Existing methods have limitations that prevent an important question from being answered.

#### Under-studied populations

Evidence comes primarily from one population, while another scientifically important population remains poorly studied.

#### Missing data

An important question cannot be adequately answered because relevant data are unavailable or insufficient.

#### Limited replication

An influential or surprising finding has limited independent verification.

#### Missing integration

Findings from different research areas have not been combined to address a meaningful question.

#### Limited application

A promising finding or method has not been adequately tested in a relevant real-world or clinical context.

---

### 5. Generate candidate gaps

For each candidate gap, record:

| Field             | Question                                  |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Gap               | What appears to be missing or unresolved? |
| Type              | What kind of gap is it?                   |
| Evidence          | Which studies support the claim?          |
| Current knowledge | What is already established?              |
| Uncertainty       | What remains unknown or disputed?         |
| Importance        | Why does resolving it matter?             |
| Recent evidence   | Has newer work addressed it?              |
| Feasibility       | Can it realistically be investigated?     |

Do not immediately select the first plausible gap.

Generate several candidates and compare them.

---

### 6. Validate the gap

This is one of the most important steps.

For every candidate gap, ask:

1. **Does the gap actually exist?**
2. **Has a recent paper already addressed it?**
3. **Is the evidence genuinely insufficient?**
4. **Is the problem important enough to investigate?**
5. **Is the apparent gap caused by insufficient searching?**
6. **Could the disagreement be explained by methodological differences?**
7. **Is the gap specific enough to investigate?**
8. **Are appropriate data and methods available?**
9. **Would resolving the gap meaningfully change scientific understanding or practice?**

A gap should survive a search for evidence that potentially closes it.

```text
Candidate gap
      ↓
Search recent literature
      ↓
Does new evidence address it?
      ├── Yes → revise or reject gap
      └── No
           ↓
      Examine evidence quality
           ↓
      Assess importance
           ↓
      Assess feasibility
           ↓
      Validated gap
```

---

### 7. Distinguish a real gap from a search failure

The following are not sufficient evidence of a research gap:

> "I could not find a paper about X."

> "Few papers mention X."

> "No paper uses method Y."

> "The authors recommend future research."

> "More research is needed."

> "This topic is interesting."

Absence from your search results does not demonstrate absence from the literature.

Before claiming a gap:

- Search multiple relevant databases or sources.
- Use alternative terminology and synonyms.
- Search recent literature.
- Check review articles.
- Check citation networks where appropriate.
- Look for preprints or newer studies when relevant.
- Search for studies using different methodological terminology.

---

### 8. Distinguish gap types carefully

Use the most scientifically meaningful classification.

Common types:

```text
Knowledge gap
Evidence gap
Methodological gap
Population gap
Data gap
Theoretical gap
Replication gap
Integration gap
Application gap
```

A single research problem may involve several.

For example:

```text
Existing evidence
      ↓
Mostly adult participants
      ↓
Few longitudinal studies
      ↓
Limited independent replication
      ↓
Uncertain developmental generalisation
```

This could involve:

- Population gap
- Evidence gap
- Replication gap

Do not list all three merely for completeness. Explain which one is the primary scientific problem.

---

### 9. Assess the importance of the gap

A gap should not be considered important simply because it is novel.

Assess:

#### Scientific importance

Would resolving it improve understanding of the phenomenon?

#### Theoretical importance

Would it distinguish between competing explanations?

#### Methodological importance

Would it resolve an important limitation in current methods?

#### Clinical or practical importance

Could the answer affect diagnosis, treatment, prediction, education, technology, or policy?

#### Generalisability

Would the result extend knowledge to an important population, context, or dataset?

#### Novelty

Does the proposed work provide information that is genuinely missing?

#### Feasibility

Can the question realistically be answered with available:

- Data
- Participants
- Equipment
- Methods
- Expertise
- Time
- Computational resources

A highly novel but impossible study is not a useful research gap.

---

### 10. Formulate the research gap

A strong gap statement normally contains:

```text
What is known
        +
What remains uncertain
        +
Why the uncertainty matters
        +
What evidence is missing
```

For example:

> Existing studies indicate that semantic information can be decoded from neuroimaging during naturalistic speech processing. However, it remains unclear how reliably these representations generalise across different narrative contexts and independent participants. This limits our understanding of whether neural semantic representations reflect stable language-related structure or context-specific processing.

Avoid exaggerated claims such as:

> "No one knows how the brain processes language."

Prefer precise claims supported by the literature.

---

### 11. Convert the gap into a research question

The research question should address the gap directly.

A useful research question should be:

- Specific
- Answerable
- Empirically testable
- Grounded in existing literature
- Feasible
- Important

The transformation should look like:

```text
Literature
    ↓
Unresolved problem
    ↓
Research gap
    ↓
Research question
    ↓
Hypothesis / objective
    ↓
Study design
```

Do not formulate the question first and then search for evidence to justify it.

---

## Evidence discipline

When making claims about the literature:

- Cite evidence supporting established findings.
- Cite studies supporting claims of disagreement.
- Cite recent work used to validate the gap.
- Distinguish an author's claim from your own synthesis.
- Record search dates when the result depends on literature recency.
- Use appropriate databases for the field.
- Do not infer absence of evidence from one source.
- Do not treat absence of evidence as evidence of absence.
- Prefer primary studies for specific empirical claims.
- Use systematic reviews and meta-analyses to understand broader evidence patterns.

When literature is incomplete, state the uncertainty explicitly.

---

## Research gap vs research limitation

Do not confuse a limitation of an individual study with a research gap.

### Study limitation

A limitation concerns a particular study.

Example:

> The study included only 20 participants.

### Research gap

A gap concerns the state of knowledge across the literature.

Example:

> Existing studies of this phenomenon generally use small samples, limiting confidence in the generalisability of the reported effect.

The second statement requires evidence across multiple studies.

---

## Research gap vs future research suggestion

A paper may state:

> "Future studies should investigate X."

This is a **candidate gap**, not automatically a validated gap.

Treat it as a hypothesis to investigate.

```text
Author's future-work suggestion
              ↓
Search literature
              ↓
Check whether later studies addressed it
              ↓
Assess importance
              ↓
Validate or reject
```

---

## Research gap vs novelty

Novelty alone does not establish a research gap.

For example:

> "No previous study has used transformer model X."

This does not automatically justify a study.

The stronger argument is:

> Existing methods have difficulty capturing long-range contextual information relevant to the research question, and transformer-based modelling may address this limitation.

The gap is the **scientific limitation**, not merely the use of a new technology.

---

## Candidate gap scoring

When multiple candidate gaps exist, compare them using:

| Criterion   | Question                                           |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Evidence    | How strongly is the gap supported?                 |
| Importance  | How much does resolving it matter?                 |
| Novelty     | Is the contribution genuinely new?                 |
| Persistence | Does the gap survive recent literature?            |
| Feasibility | Can it realistically be investigated?              |
| Clarity     | Can it be expressed precisely?                     |
| Impact      | Could the answer change understanding or practice? |

Use qualitative ratings such as:

- High
- Medium
- Low

Do not reduce the decision to a numerical score unless there is a clear reason to do so.

---

## Output format

When asked to identify a research gap, structure the analysis as follows.

### 1. Research area

Define the topic and scope.

### 2. Current evidence

Summarise what is established.

### 3. Candidate gaps

For each candidate:

- Gap statement
- Gap type
- Supporting evidence
- Current uncertainty
- Why it matters
- Recent validation
- Feasibility

### 4. Best-supported gap

Identify the strongest candidate and explain why it is more defensible than the alternatives.

### 5. Research question

Convert the gap into one or more researchable questions.

### 6. Evidence confidence

Classify the gap:

- **High confidence**
- **Moderate confidence**
- **Preliminary**

Explain the basis for the classification.

### 7. Remaining uncertainty

State what additional evidence would be needed to strengthen or weaken the gap.

---

## Quality checklist

Before accepting a research gap:

- [ ] The research scope is clearly defined.
- [ ] Relevant literature has been mapped.
- [ ] Established findings are separated from assumptions.
- [ ] Conflicting evidence has been identified.
- [ ] Candidate gaps are supported by evidence.
- [ ] Recent literature has been checked.
- [ ] The gap is not merely a future-work suggestion.
- [ ] The gap is not simply a search failure.
- [ ] The gap is not based solely on novelty.
- [ ] The scientific importance is explained.
- [ ] The gap is specific enough to investigate.
- [ ] The gap is feasible to investigate.
- [ ] The gap can be converted into a research question.
- [ ] Claims about the literature are appropriately supported.

## Final mental model

```text
WHAT DO WE KNOW?
        ↓
HOW STRONG IS THE EVIDENCE?
        ↓
WHERE DO STUDIES DISAGREE?
        ↓
WHAT IS MISSING?
        ↓
WHY DOES IT MATTER?
        ↓
DOES THE GAP STILL EXIST?
        ↓
CAN WE TEST IT?
        ↓
RESEARCH GAP
        ↓
RESEARCH QUESTION
```

The objective is not to find the most novel claim.

The objective is to identify the **most defensible important question that the existing literature has not adequately answered**.
