# Research Gap Example: Psychology

## Purpose

This example demonstrates how to identify, validate, and formulate a research gap in psychology.

The example focuses on cognitive training and working memory, but the same reasoning can be applied to:

- Social psychology
- Developmental psychology
- Clinical psychology
- Cognitive psychology
- Educational psychology
- Personality psychology
- Behavioural science

The example is intentionally simplified. It demonstrates the reasoning process rather than representing a complete systematic review.

---

# 1. Initial topic

Suppose the research topic is:

> Does working-memory training improve cognitive performance?

This is already more specific than a broad topic such as:

> Cognitive training.

However, it is still too broad.

It could involve:

- Children
- Older adults
- Healthy adults
- Clinical populations
- Working-memory tasks
- Attention tasks
- Intelligence
- Academic performance
- Everyday functioning
- Short-term effects
- Long-term effects
- Near transfer
- Far transfer

The first step is therefore to define the scope.

---

# 2. Define the scope

Suppose we focus on:

```text
Population:
Healthy adults

Intervention:
Working-memory training

Comparison:
Active control

Primary outcomes:
Working-memory performance

Secondary outcomes:
Attention and reasoning

Question:
Does training transfer beyond the trained task?
```

The research problem is now substantially narrower.

---

# 3. Initial literature map

Imagine the literature contains:

```text
Study A
Working-memory training
        ↓
Improved trained task

Study B
Working-memory training
        ↓
Improved related working-memory task

Study C
Working-memory training
        ↓
No improvement in reasoning

Study D
Working-memory training
        ↓
Small improvement in attention

Study E
Working-memory training
        ↓
No long-term benefit
```

The literature therefore appears mixed.

This disagreement is potentially important.

---

# 4. Establish what is known

A synthesis might be:

> Working-memory training reliably improves performance on tasks that are similar to the trained tasks, but evidence for broader transfer to untrained cognitive abilities is less consistent.

This is much more informative than listing each study separately.

The important distinction is:

```text
Near transfer
    ↓
More consistent

Far transfer
    ↓
Less consistent
```

---

# 5. Identify candidate gaps

Several possible gaps emerge.

### Candidate A: Does working-memory training work?

This question may already have substantial evidence.

### Candidate B: Does training produce far transfer?

This has more uncertainty.

### Candidate C: Which people benefit most?

This concerns individual differences.

### Candidate D: How long do benefits persist?

This concerns durability.

### Candidate E: Which mechanisms explain transfer?

This concerns mechanism.

Each candidate requires separate validation.

---

# 6. Candidate gap A: basic efficacy

Candidate:

> It is unknown whether working-memory training improves cognitive performance.

Suppose multiple meta-analyses and controlled trials have already demonstrated improvements on trained or closely related tasks.

### Assessment

```text
Status: Largely addressed
```

This is therefore not a strong research gap.

A new study asking the same broad question would add limited knowledge unless it addressed an important unresolved issue.

---

# 7. Candidate gap B: far transfer

Candidate:

> It remains uncertain whether working-memory training produces reliable improvements in unrelated cognitive abilities.

This is more promising.

The key distinction is:

```text
Training task
      ↓
Similar task
      ↓
Different cognitive ability
```

The further the outcome is from the trained task, the stronger the claim about transfer.

For example:

```text
Train:
Working-memory updating

Near transfer:
Another working-memory task

Far transfer:
Reasoning task
```

---

# 8. Validate the far-transfer gap

The literature should be examined for:

- Meta-analyses
- Large randomised controlled trials
- Active-control studies
- Preregistered studies
- Long-term follow-ups
- Different training paradigms
- Different outcome measures
- Publication bias
- Selective outcome reporting

Suppose the evidence shows:

```text
Trained task
██████████████████ strong evidence

Near transfer
██████████████ moderate/strong evidence

Far transfer
██████ mixed evidence
```

This suggests genuine uncertainty.

However, "mixed evidence" alone is not enough.

The next question is:

> Why is the evidence mixed?

---

# 9. Investigate the disagreement

Possible explanations include:

### Training differences

- Training duration
- Number of sessions
- Adaptive vs fixed difficulty
- Training task
- Feedback

### Participant differences

- Age
- Baseline ability
- Motivation
- Sleep
- Education

### Outcome differences

- Different reasoning measures
- Different attention measures
- Different working-memory tasks

### Methodological differences

- Active vs passive controls
- Blinding
- Sample size
- Preregistration
- Attrition
- Statistical analysis

### Reporting differences

- Selective reporting
- Publication bias
- Multiple outcomes

The research gap may therefore be more specific than simply:

> Evidence is mixed.

---

# 10. Example of gap refinement

Suppose the literature reveals:

```text
Passive-control studies
        ↓
Often report transfer

Active-control studies
        ↓
Much smaller effects

Short-term outcomes
        ↓
Some evidence

Long-term outcomes
        ↓
Limited evidence
```

The original gap:

> Does working-memory training improve cognition?

is too broad.

A stronger gap becomes:

> It remains unclear whether working-memory training produces meaningful far-transfer benefits when compared with active control conditions, particularly when outcomes are assessed beyond the immediate post-training period.

This is substantially more precise.

---

# 11. Candidate gap: individual differences

Another possibility is:

> Training effects vary substantially between individuals, but the factors explaining this variation are unclear.

This can be investigated by asking whether outcomes depend on:

- Baseline working-memory ability
- Age
- Motivation
- Training adherence
- Personality
- Cognitive strategy
- Sleep
- Neurobiological measures

The research gap might therefore become:

> Existing studies provide inconsistent evidence about which individual characteristics predict responsiveness to working-memory training.

This is an **individual-differences gap**, rather than a basic efficacy gap.

---

# 12. Candidate gap: durability

Suppose many studies measure outcomes immediately after training.

```text
Pre-training
    ↓
Training
    ↓
Immediate post-test
    ↓
Result
```

Far fewer studies measure:

```text
Pre-training
    ↓
Training
    ↓
Immediate post-test
    ↓
3 months
    ↓
6 months
    ↓
12 months
```

A candidate gap is:

> It remains unclear whether observed cognitive benefits persist after training ends.

But again, search for recent longitudinal evidence before accepting this as a gap.

A recent large study could partially close it.

---

# 13. Candidate gap: mechanism

Another possible question is:

> Why does training transfer?

Possible mechanisms include:

- Improved attentional control
- Improved strategy use
- Increased task familiarity
- Improved metacognition
- Improved motivation
- General cognitive adaptation

A successful behavioural result does not automatically identify the mechanism.

Therefore:

```text
Training causes improvement
        ≠
We know why improvement occurred
```

A mechanistic gap may be particularly valuable if several competing explanations exist.

---

# 14. Compare candidate gaps

| Candidate              | Evidence of uncertainty | Importance | Novelty     | Feasibility |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| Basic efficacy         | Low                     | High       | Low         | High        |
| Far transfer           | High                    | High       | Medium/High | High        |
| Individual differences | High                    | High       | High        | Medium      |
| Long-term durability   | High                    | High       | High        | Medium      |
| Mechanism              | High                    | High       | High        | Medium/Low  |

The best gap is not necessarily the most novel.

It should balance:

```text
Evidence
+
Importance
+
Novelty
+
Feasibility
```

---

# 15. Search for evidence against the gap

Suppose the candidate gap is:

> Long-term effects of working-memory training are poorly understood.

Do not search only for studies supporting this statement.

Search directly for evidence that could disprove it:

```text
"working memory training" longitudinal
"working memory training" follow-up
"working memory training" 6 month
"working memory training" 12 month
"working memory training" maintenance
"working memory training" long-term effects
```

The goal is adversarial validation.

Ask:

> What evidence would show that my proposed gap is already closed?

---

# 16. Recent evidence can narrow the gap

Suppose a recent large trial reports:

> Working-memory training effects remain detectable six months later.

The broad durability gap is now weakened.

But perhaps the study:

- Uses one training protocol
- Studies only young adults
- Uses one outcome
- Has no active control
- Measures only one follow-up point

The gap could therefore become:

> Evidence for long-term maintenance exists for specific training protocols, but it remains uncertain whether such effects generalise across training paradigms and persist across multiple cognitive outcomes under active-control conditions.

This is a much stronger research gap.

---

# 17. Distinguish statistical significance from meaningful benefit

Psychology research often produces statistically significant effects that may have limited practical importance.

Suppose:

```text
Effect:
p < .001

Standardised effect:
d = 0.10
```

A statistically reliable effect does not necessarily imply a meaningful cognitive improvement.

Therefore, when evaluating a gap, ask:

- How large is the effect?
- Is it practically meaningful?
- Does it exceed measurement error?
- Does it transfer to real-world functioning?
- Is the outcome clinically or educationally meaningful?

This can reveal an **interpretation gap**.

---

# 18. Measurement can create a research gap

Suppose a study claims:

> Working-memory training improves attention.

But the attention outcome is measured using a single laboratory task.

The question becomes:

> Does improvement on this task represent genuine improvement in attention, or task-specific learning?

This is a measurement problem.

Possible gap:

> Evidence for transfer to attention may depend strongly on the specific outcome measure used, limiting conclusions about whether training improves the broader construct of attentional control.

This is stronger than simply saying:

> More attention studies are needed.

---

# 19. Example of a validated gap

After reviewing the evidence, suppose the strongest conclusion is:

> Working-memory training reliably improves performance on trained and closely related tasks. Evidence for far transfer to broader cognitive abilities is considerably less consistent, particularly when studies use active control conditions. Existing studies also vary substantially in training protocols and outcome measures, making it difficult to determine whether observed effects represent general cognitive improvement or task-specific learning.

This identifies several unresolved issues.

The most focused gap might be:

> It remains unclear whether working-memory training produces meaningful far-transfer effects beyond task-specific learning when compared with an active control and assessed using multiple measures of the target cognitive construct.

---

# 20. Convert the gap into a research question

A corresponding question could be:

> Does adaptive working-memory training produce greater improvement in untrained attentional control than an active control condition?

This is answerable because it specifies:

```text
Population
    ↓
Adults

Intervention
    ↓
Adaptive working-memory training

Comparison
    ↓
Active control

Outcome
    ↓
Untrained attentional control
```

---

# 21. Possible secondary question

A secondary question could be:

> Are individual differences in baseline working-memory ability associated with the magnitude of transfer to attentional control?

This should remain secondary if the primary question is about whether transfer exists.

Avoid combining too many questions into one:

> Does training work, for whom, why, how long, in which tasks, and under which conditions?

That becomes difficult to answer with one study.

---

# 22. Possible hypothesis

If prior evidence supports a directional prediction:

> Participants receiving adaptive working-memory training will show greater improvement on untrained measures of attentional control than participants receiving an active control intervention.

A moderation hypothesis could be:

> Baseline working-memory ability will moderate the magnitude of transfer to attentional control.

But moderation hypotheses should be justified by previous evidence rather than added simply to make the study more sophisticated.

---

# 23. Example: developmental psychology

The same approach can identify gaps in developmental psychology.

Suppose the topic is:

> Language development in children.

A weak gap:

> More research is needed on language development.

A stronger candidate:

> Most studies measure language development at isolated time points, limiting understanding of how changes in vocabulary, syntax, and conversational complexity interact over development.

Potential question:

> How do changes in vocabulary and syntactic complexity interact longitudinally during early language development?

The key issue is not simply that children have been understudied.

It is that the **relationship between developmental processes remains unresolved**.

---

# 24. Example: social psychology

Suppose the topic is:

> Social media and wellbeing.

A weak gap:

> The effects of social media on mental health are unclear.

A stronger gap might emerge after separating:

```text
Passive use
vs
Active interaction
vs
Social comparison
vs
Messaging
```

The literature may show that "social media use" is too heterogeneous to support a single conclusion.

A potential methodological gap is:

> Studies often aggregate qualitatively different forms of social media engagement, making it difficult to determine which specific behaviours are associated with changes in wellbeing.

Potential research question:

> How do passive social media consumption and active interpersonal interaction differ in their association with short-term wellbeing?

---

# 25. Example: clinical psychology

Suppose the topic is:

> Psychological treatment for anxiety.

A weak gap:

> More effective treatments are needed.

A stronger gap:

> Average treatment effects are well established, but it remains uncertain which patient characteristics predict differential response across treatment approaches.

This becomes an individual-differences question:

> Which baseline characteristics predict differential response to cognitive behavioural therapy versus another evidence-based intervention?

The gap concerns **heterogeneity of treatment response**, not basic treatment efficacy.

---

# 26. Important psychology-specific considerations

When evaluating a psychology research gap, pay particular attention to:

### Construct validity

Does the measurement actually represent the psychological construct?

```text
Task performance
      ≠
Psychological construct
```

### Measurement reliability

Unreliable measures can make genuine effects difficult to detect.

### Demand characteristics

Could participants change behaviour because they know what the study expects?

### Placebo and expectancy effects

Especially important for interventions.

### Active controls

A passive control may not account for:

- Expectancy
- Contact time
- Motivation
- Practice
- Social interaction

### Sample characteristics

Consider:

- Age
- Sex/gender
- Education
- Culture
- Clinical status
- Recruitment method

### Generalisability

Ask whether findings generalise beyond the original sample.

### Replication

A finding supported by one study may represent a replication gap rather than a completely new theoretical gap.

---

# 27. What this example demonstrates

The progression is:

```text
Broad psychological topic
        ↓
Define construct and population
        ↓
Map existing findings
        ↓
Separate near and far effects
        ↓
Identify disagreement
        ↓
Investigate methodological differences
        ↓
Search for recent evidence
        ↓
Challenge candidate gaps
        ↓
Narrow partially closed gaps
        ↓
Validated research gap
        ↓
Research question
```

---

# 28. Key lessons

### Lesson 1

A mixed literature is not automatically a research gap.

First determine **why** findings disagree.

### Lesson 2

Psychological constructs must be distinguished from the tasks used to measure them.

### Lesson 3

Statistical significance does not establish practical significance.

### Lesson 4

Active controls can substantially change the interpretation of intervention effects.

### Lesson 5

Individual differences can reveal important gaps hidden by average effects.

### Lesson 6

Long-term effects should not be inferred from immediate post-test effects.

### Lesson 7

A broad claim such as "social media affects wellbeing" may conceal several fundamentally different behaviours.

### Lesson 8

The strongest question often targets the reason for disagreement rather than simply repeating the original comparison.

---

# Final mental model

For psychology research-gap identification:

```text
PSYCHOLOGICAL CONSTRUCT
          ↓
      WHAT IS KNOWN?
          ↓
    HOW IS IT MEASURED?
          ↓
   HOW RELIABLE IS IT?
          ↓
   WHERE DO RESULTS AGREE?
          ↓
   WHERE DO RESULTS DISAGREE?
          ↓
      WHY?
          ↓
  SAMPLE / METHOD / MEASURE /
  CONTEXT / MODERATOR / TIME
          ↓
  WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAIN?
          ↓
  WHY DOES IT MATTER?
          ↓
    VALIDATED GAP
          ↓
  RESEARCH QUESTION
```

The key question is:

> **"What important psychological conclusion does the current evidence still not allow us to make?"**
