# Scientific Writing

## Purpose

This reference provides general principles for clear, precise, and effective scientific writing.

It should be used when drafting or reviewing research manuscripts, reports, dissertations, and other scientific documents.

---

## 1. Scientific Writing Is Scientific Communication

Scientific writing is not simply formal prose.

Its primary purpose is to communicate:

1. What question was investigated.
2. Why the question matters.
3. What was done.
4. What was found.
5. What the findings mean.
6. What can and cannot be concluded.

Good scientific writing therefore prioritises:

- Accuracy
- Clarity
- Precision
- Reproducibility
- Appropriate qualification
- Logical structure

Elegant writing is useful, but it should never come at the expense of scientific accuracy.

---

## 2. Make the Research Question Central

A research article should have a clear intellectual focus.

The research question should connect the major sections:

```text
Research question
       ↓
Study design
       ↓
Analysis
       ↓
Results
       ↓
Interpretation
       ↓
Conclusion
```

When reviewing a manuscript, ask:

> Can I identify the research question?

Then ask:

> Can I see how every major section contributes to answering it?

Material that does not contribute to the research question should be reconsidered.

---

## 3. Establish a Research Gap

A strong Introduction generally moves from established knowledge toward an unresolved problem.

A useful pattern is:

```text
What is known?
      ↓
What is not known?
      ↓
Why does the gap matter?
      ↓
What will this study investigate?
```

The research gap should not be manufactured simply to justify a study.

A valid gap may involve:

- Missing evidence
- Conflicting findings
- An understudied population
- An unresolved mechanism
- A methodological limitation
- An unexplored application
- An insufficiently tested theoretical prediction

---

## 4. Build a Logical Argument

Scientific writing should form an argument rather than a collection of facts.

A paragraph should contribute to the larger argument.

A useful structure is:

```text
Claim
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Reasoning
   ↓
Implication
```

For example:

```text
Claim:
Naturalistic stimuli may provide more ecologically valid measures
of language processing.

Evidence:
Previous studies using naturalistic paradigms have demonstrated
reliable neural responses during continuous speech.

Reasoning:
Continuous stimuli preserve temporal and contextual information
that may be absent from isolated-word paradigms.

Implication:
Naturalistic paradigms may therefore be useful for investigating
language processing in more realistic conditions.
```

The exact structure will vary by paragraph, but the logical relationship should remain clear.

---

## 5. Distinguish Facts, Results, Interpretations, and Speculation

Scientific writing contains different levels of epistemic certainty.

### Established evidence

> Previous studies have reported...

### Current observation

> We observed...

### Interpretation

> This finding suggests...

### Hypothesis or speculation

> One possibility is that...

These categories should not be blurred.

A common writing error is to convert:

> The data are consistent with X.

into:

> The data demonstrate X.

The second statement makes a stronger claim.

---

## 6. Use Appropriate Scientific Language

Prefer precise language over unnecessarily complicated language.

Weak:

> It is important to note that the findings obtained from the present investigation potentially provide evidence that...

Better:

> These findings provide evidence that...

Or, when appropriate:

> These findings suggest that...

Avoid inflated academic language.

Scientific writing does not become more rigorous by becoming more complicated.

---

## 7. Prefer Specific Statements

Vague:

> Brain activity was affected by the task.

More precise:

> Task difficulty was associated with increased activity in the frontoparietal network.

More precise still, when supported:

> Mean activity in the predefined frontoparietal regions was higher during the high-difficulty condition than during the low-difficulty condition.

The appropriate level of specificity depends on the evidence available.

---

## 8. Avoid Ambiguous Pronouns

Scientific writing can become difficult to interpret when pronouns have unclear references.

Weak:

> This suggests that it affects the process.

Better:

> This finding suggests that task difficulty affects response selection.

Name the relevant object when precision matters.

---

## 9. Maintain Terminological Consistency

Use the same term for the same concept unless there is a meaningful reason to distinguish them.

For example, if a manuscript defines:

> reaction time (RT)

then use `RT` consistently rather than alternating between:

- reaction time
- response time
- response latency
- reaction latency

unless these terms genuinely refer to different variables.

Define abbreviations at first use when required by the target venue.

---

## 10. Avoid Unnecessary Nominalisation

Nominalisation converts verbs or adjectives into nouns.

Examples:

- analyse → analysis
- investigate → investigation
- determine → determination
- assess → assessment

Nominalisation is not inherently bad, but excessive use can make sentences harder to read.

Weak:

> The analysis of the relationship between task difficulty and reaction time was performed.

Clearer:

> We analysed the relationship between task difficulty and reaction time.

Use the clearest grammatical structure rather than avoiding nominalisation mechanically.

---

## 11. Use Active and Passive Voice Deliberately

Active voice can make scientific writing direct:

> We analysed the MEG recordings.

Passive voice can be useful when the procedure is more important than the researcher:

> The MEG recordings were filtered at 1–40 Hz.

Neither voice is inherently more scientific.

Choose based on clarity, convention, and the requirements of the target journal.

---

## 12. Avoid Overclaiming

Common examples of overclaiming include:

### Correlation → causation

Weak:

> Increased activity in region X causes better performance.

If the study only measured correlation, write:

> Increased activity in region X was associated with better performance.

### Statistical significance → importance

Weak:

> The intervention had an important effect because p < .05.

Better:

> The intervention produced a statistically significant difference, with an estimated effect size of X.

Interpret practical importance separately.

### Prediction → explanation

Weak:

> The model explains human decision-making.

Better:

> The model predicts participants' response times.

Predictive performance and explanatory adequacy are different claims.

---

## 13. Write With Uncertainty

Scientific knowledge is often uncertain.

Useful terms include:

- may
- might
- could
- suggests
- is consistent with
- provides evidence for
- is compatible with
- raises the possibility

However, excessive hedging can make writing vague.

Compare:

> The results may possibly suggest that there could potentially be an association.

with:

> The results suggest an association.

Use the strongest language that is justified by the evidence.

---

## 14. Structure the Introduction as a Funnel

A common Introduction structure is:

```text
Broad field
    ↓
Specific topic
    ↓
Relevant previous findings
    ↓
Limitation / unresolved issue
    ↓
Research gap
    ↓
Research question
    ↓
Hypothesis / objective
```

Each paragraph should move the reader closer to the study's specific question.

Avoid turning the Introduction into a chronological list of papers.

The goal is synthesis, not bibliography.

---

## 15. Structure the Discussion Around Meaning

A Discussion should not simply repeat the Results.

A useful structure is:

```text
Main finding
    ↓
Meaning
    ↓
Relationship to previous research
    ↓
Possible explanation
    ↓
Implications
    ↓
Limitations
    ↓
Future research
    ↓
Conclusion
```

The Discussion should answer:

> What do these findings contribute to our understanding of the research question?

---

## 16. Use Literature as an Argument

Avoid writing:

> Smith et al. found X.  
> Jones et al. found Y.  
> Brown et al. found Z.

This creates a list rather than an argument.

Prefer synthesis:

> Previous studies generally indicate X, although findings differ regarding Y. Smith et al. reported X under condition A, whereas Jones et al. observed a different pattern under condition B. These differences may reflect variation in task demands or measurement methods.

The purpose of citations is to build and support an argument.

---

## 17. Distinguish Primary and Secondary Sources

### Primary sources

Original research containing:

- Experimental data
- Original analyses
- New datasets
- New methods
- Original theoretical contributions

### Secondary sources

Sources that synthesise existing research:

- Review articles
- Systematic reviews
- Meta-analyses
- Textbooks

Use the source type appropriate to the claim.

When making a specific empirical claim, prefer the original research where feasible.

---

## 18. Make Methods Reproducible

A reader should be able to determine:

- What data were collected or used
- Who or what was studied
- What procedures were performed
- How the data were processed
- What analyses were conducted
- What statistical criteria were used

For computational research, reproducibility may additionally require:

- Software
- Package versions
- Model specifications
- Parameter settings
- Random seeds where relevant
- Data preprocessing decisions
- Code or repository information where available

Do not omit decisions simply because they seem obvious to the researcher.

---

## 19. Report Results Without Interpretation

The Results section should establish what the analysis found.

A useful reporting sequence is:

```text
Question / analysis
      ↓
Method or comparison
      ↓
Estimate
      ↓
Uncertainty / statistical result
      ↓
Relevant figure or table
```

Interpretation should generally be reserved for the Discussion.

However, Results writing can still be selective: report the findings relevant to the research questions rather than every output produced by an analysis pipeline.

---

## 20. Use Figures as Part of the Argument

A figure should have a communicative purpose.

Before including a figure, ask:

> What should the reader learn from this figure?

The text should identify the important pattern.

Do not force the reader to interpret a complex figure without guidance.

Captions should provide enough information to understand the figure's purpose, variables, and relevant conditions.

---

## 21. Revise in Multiple Passes

A useful revision order is:

### Pass 1 — Argument

Check:

- Research question
- Research gap
- Logic
- Evidence
- Conclusions

### Pass 2 — Structure

Check:

- Section organisation
- Paragraph order
- Transitions
- Redundancy

### Pass 3 — Scientific precision

Check:

- Terminology
- Statistics
- Methodological claims
- Causal language
- Uncertainty

### Pass 4 — Citations

Check:

- Source relevance
- Source accuracy
- Primary versus secondary sources
- Citation completeness

### Pass 5 — Language

Check:

- Grammar
- Sentence structure
- Word choice
- Concision
- Formatting

Scientific writing should be revised from the highest-level reasoning down to sentence-level language.

---

## 22. Final Principle

The most important rule is:

> **Write exactly what the evidence supports—no more and no less.**

Good scientific writing makes the reasoning visible:

```text
Question
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Analysis
   ↓
Finding
   ↓
Interpretation
   ↓
Conclusion
```

The reader should be able to follow this chain without having to reconstruct the argument themselves.
