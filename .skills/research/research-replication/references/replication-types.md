# Replication Types

## Purpose

Replication studies can differ substantially in what they preserve, what they change, and what scientific question they answer.

The term **replication** therefore should not be treated as a single study design.

This reference helps determine:

- What type of replication is being conducted
- What should remain constant
- What may change
- What claim is being tested
- What conclusions are justified

The central principle is:

> **Classify a replication by the scientific relationship between the original and new study, not simply by how similar their procedures look.**

---

# 1. The replication spectrum

Replication designs can be viewed as a spectrum:

```text
More methodologically similar
            ↓
Direct
            ↓
Close
            ↓
Extended
            ↓
Conceptual
            ↓
More methodologically different
```

However, methodological similarity alone does not determine replication quality.

A highly similar study can still be scientifically weak if:

- It reuses the same participants
- It uses overlapping data
- The primary claim is unclear
- The analysis changes after seeing the data

Likewise, a less similar study can be a strong conceptual replication if it tests the same underlying theoretical claim.

---

# 2. Direct replication

## Definition

A direct replication attempts to reproduce the essential features of the original study while collecting new evidence.

Conceptually:

```text
Original study
      ↓
Same core design
      ↓
New evidence
      ↓
Same scientific test
```

The objective is:

> Does the original finding appear again under closely matched conditions?

---

## Typical characteristics

A direct replication attempts to preserve:

- Population characteristics
- Experimental manipulation
- Control condition
- Stimuli
- Procedure
- Primary outcome
- Timing
- Analysis
- Statistical test

Some practical differences are usually unavoidable.

---

## Example: psychology

Original:

```text
Participants:
University students

Manipulation:
Priming condition

Control:
Neutral condition

Outcome:
Reaction time

Finding:
Priming reduces reaction time
```

Replication:

```text
New students
Same priming manipulation
Same control
Equivalent reaction-time task
Predefined analysis
```

The central scientific test is preserved.

---

## Example: neuroscience

Original:

```text
Participants:
Healthy adults

Task:
Semantic decision

Modality:
fMRI

Primary contrast:
Semantic > control

Finding:
Increased activity in region X
```

Replication:

```text
New healthy adults
Same task
Equivalent fMRI acquisition
Same contrast
Predefined ROI/statistical analysis
```

---

## Strength

A direct replication is useful for determining whether a finding survives a closely matched new test.

---

## Limitation

A successful direct replication does not establish that the finding generalises to:

- Other populations
- Other tasks
- Other laboratories
- Other contexts
- Other measurement methods

---

# 3. Close replication

## Definition

A close replication preserves the essential scientific design while allowing practical or scientifically justified changes.

```text
Original
   ↓
Essential components preserved
   ↓
Some implementation differences
   ↓
New evidence
```

Examples of reasonable changes:

- Different laboratory
- Updated software
- Different recruitment site
- Equivalent stimulus materials
- Improved equipment
- Minor procedural changes

---

## Example

Original:

```text
MATLAB
Version X
```

Replication:

```text
MATLAB
Version Y
```

If the version change does not alter the scientific test, this may remain a close replication.

---

# 4. Essential versus flexible components

Before designing a close replication, divide the original study into two groups.

## Essential components

These define the scientific test.

Examples:

```text
Population
Critical manipulation
Comparison
Primary outcome
Timing
Core task
Primary contrast
```

## Flexible components

These may change without changing the scientific meaning.

Examples:

```text
Software version
Laboratory location
Computer hardware
Interface appearance
Non-critical stimulus formatting
```

The classification should be justified rather than assumed.

---

# 5. Conceptual replication

## Definition

A conceptual replication tests the same underlying hypothesis using a substantially different operationalisation.

```text
Original operationalisation
        ↓
Underlying theoretical claim
        ↓
Different operationalisation
        ↓
New evidence
```

The goal is:

> Does the theoretical relationship hold when measured differently?

---

## Example: psychology

Original:

```text
Working-memory training
        ↓
Digit-span improvement
```

Conceptual replication:

```text
Working-memory training
        ↓
Complex-span improvement
```

The specific measurement changes, but the broader hypothesis concerns improvement in working-memory ability.

---

## Example: neuroscience

Original:

```text
Semantic processing
        ↓
fMRI activation
        ↓
Region X
```

Conceptual replication:

```text
Semantic processing
        ↓
MEG response
        ↓
Temporal neural dynamics
```

The modality changes.

The theoretical question may remain:

> Does semantic processing produce a characteristic neural representation?

---

## Important caution

A conceptual replication must preserve the theoretical meaning.

Changing:

```text
Semantic processing
```

into:

```text
Speech category classification
```

may be a different scientific question even though both involve language and neural data.

---

# 6. Extended replication

## Definition

An extended replication tests the original claim while adding a scientifically motivated component.

```text
Original test
     +
New condition
     ↓
Extended replication
```

The original claim remains identifiable.

---

## Example: population

Original:

```text
Young adults
```

Replication:

```text
Young adults
+
Older adults
```

The original population provides the replication test.

The older group provides an extension.

---

## Example: context

Original:

```text
Laboratory environment
```

Replication:

```text
Laboratory
+
Naturalistic environment
```

This can test whether the finding survives a more ecologically realistic context.

---

## Example: neuroscience

Original:

```text
EEG
```

Extended replication:

```text
EEG
+
fMRI
```

The behavioural or cognitive claim can be replicated while the additional modality tests a complementary neural question.

---

# 7. Multi-site replication

## Definition

A multi-site replication tests a finding across multiple independent research sites.

```text
Site A ─┐
Site B ─┤
Site C ─┼──→ Combined replication evidence
Site D ─┘
```

The purpose is to evaluate robustness to:

- Laboratory
- Experimenter
- Equipment
- Recruitment
- Local population
- Data-collection procedures

---

## Why it matters

A finding observed at one laboratory may partly depend on:

```text
Local recruitment
Equipment
Experimenter
Population
Protocol
```

Multi-site designs test whether the finding survives these sources of variation.

---

## Example: clinical neuroscience

Original:

```text
Hospital A
```

Replication:

```text
Hospital A
Hospital B
Hospital C
Hospital D
```

This provides stronger evidence about clinical generalisability than repeating the study at the same institution alone.

---

# 8. Cross-population replication

## Definition

Tests whether a finding holds in a meaningfully different population.

Examples:

```text
Adults → Children

Young adults → Older adults

Healthy → Clinical

One language → Another language

One cultural group → Another
```

---

## Important distinction

Cross-population replication is simultaneously a test of:

```text
Replication
+
Generalisability
```

Therefore a failure does not necessarily invalidate the original finding.

It may indicate:

```text
Population moderation
```

---

# 9. Cross-context replication

## Definition

Tests the original claim under a different context.

Examples:

```text
Laboratory → Field

One task → Another equivalent task

One narrative → Another narrative

One session → Multiple sessions

One time point → Different time point
```

The central scientific claim should remain identifiable.

---

## Example: language neuroscience

Original:

```text
Controlled sentence comprehension
```

Cross-context replication:

```text
Naturalistic narrative comprehension
```

The study may ask whether the neural relationship generalises from controlled language processing to naturalistic language.

This should be described as both replication and generalisation if appropriate.

---

# 10. Cross-method replication

## Definition

Tests the same scientific claim using a different measurement method.

Examples:

```text
fMRI → MEG

EEG → OPM-MEG

Self-report → Behavioural task

Behavioural measure → Physiological measure
```

The important question is:

> Does the method change the scientific construct being tested?

If yes, the new study may be a different scientific question rather than a replication.

---

# 11. Cross-measurement replication

Sometimes the underlying construct is retained but the measurement changes.

Example:

```text
Original:
Anxiety questionnaire A

Replication:
Anxiety questionnaire B
```

If both measure the same construct with sufficient validity, this can provide a useful conceptual replication.

However, measurement reliability must be considered.

A failed replication could result from:

```text
True effect absent
```

or:

```text
New measurement unreliable
```

---

# 12. Cross-dataset replication

Particularly important for computational research and machine learning.

```text
Original dataset
      ↓
Original finding

Independent dataset
      ↓
Replication
```

Examples:

- Different cohort
- Different hospital
- Different scanner
- Different country
- Different recording session
- Different benchmark dataset

---

# 13. External validation as replication evidence

For predictive models:

```text
Training dataset
       ↓
Model
       ↓
Independent test dataset
       ↓
Performance
```

External validation can provide replication-like evidence about model performance.

But distinguish:

```text
Model performance replication
```

from:

```text
Replication of the scientific mechanism
```

A model may generalise without the model's internal features representing the proposed mechanism.

---

# 14. Registered replication

A registered replication specifies important decisions before data collection or analysis.

Possible preregistered elements:

```text
Hypothesis
Sample
Primary outcome
Exclusions
Analysis
Effect measure
Stopping rule
```

A registered replication can reduce:

- HARKing
- Selective analysis
- Outcome switching
- Flexible stopping
- Post-hoc hypothesis formation

---

# 15. Registered Reports

A Registered Report separates study evaluation from study results.

Conceptually:

```text
Research question
      ↓
Study protocol
      ↓
Peer review
      ↓
Acceptance in principle
      ↓
Data collection
      ↓
Analysis
      ↓
Publication
```

This can reduce incentives to produce statistically significant findings.

A Registered Report is a publication format, not a replication type itself.

A Registered Report can contain:

- A replication
- An original study
- A conceptual replication
- A methodological study

---

# 16. Replication-by-extension

Some studies deliberately combine replication with extension.

Structure:

```text
Original claim
      ↓
Replication condition
      +
Extension condition
      ↓
New evidence
```

Example:

```text
Original:
Language decoding in adults

Replication:
Language decoding in new adults

Extension:
Language decoding in children
```

This allows the original claim and the new research question to be evaluated separately.

---

# 17. Same-site versus independent-site replication

These should be distinguished.

### Same-site

```text
Original:
University A

Replication:
University A
```

Advantages:

- Easier methodological matching
- Familiar equipment
- Easier protocol transfer

Limitations:

- May preserve site-specific biases
- Less evidence about laboratory generalisation

### Independent-site

```text
Original:
University A

Replication:
University B
```

Advantages:

- Greater environmental independence
- Tests robustness across researchers/sites

Limitations:

- More procedural variation

Neither is universally better.

They answer somewhat different questions.

---

# 18. Same-population versus new-population replication

### Same population

Tests:

> Does the finding hold again in a comparable population?

### New population

Tests:

> Does the finding generalise beyond the original population?

Do not describe the second as merely a stronger version of the first.

They address different uncertainties.

---

# 19. Same-method versus new-method replication

### Same method

Useful for:

```text
Direct robustness
```

### New method

Useful for:

```text
Construct robustness
```

Example:

```text
Original:
Behavioural reaction time

New:
EEG neural response
```

This does not automatically test exactly the same claim.

The construct and inference chain must be examined.

---

# 20. Replication hierarchy

There is no universally correct hierarchy, but a useful conceptual progression is:

```text
Same participants
      ↓
Same dataset
      ↓
NEW DATA
      ↓
Same population
      ↓
Different site
      ↓
Different population
      ↓
Different context
      ↓
Different method
      ↓
Different operationalisation
```

As more dimensions change, the study tests broader generalisation.

But increasing methodological difference also makes discrepancies harder to interpret.

---

# 21. The fidelity-generalisation trade-off

Replication design often involves a trade-off:

```text
High fidelity
    ↓
Better test of original result
    ↓
Lower generalisation

High variation
    ↓
Better test of generalisation
    ↓
Harder to attribute discrepancies
```

Therefore choose the design based on the scientific question.

---

# 22. Replication design matrix

A useful planning matrix is:

| Dimension    | Original     | Replication  | Purpose                    |
| ------------ | ------------ | ------------ | -------------------------- |
| Participants | Students     | New students | Replication                |
| Site         | Lab A        | Lab B        | Site independence          |
| Task         | Task X       | Task X       | Fidelity                   |
| Outcome      | Measure Y    | Measure Y    | Fidelity                   |
| Population   | Young adults | Older adults | Generalisation             |
| Method       | fMRI         | fMRI         | Methodological consistency |
| Context      | Laboratory   | Laboratory   | Fidelity                   |

This makes the intended replication explicit.

---

# 23. How to choose the replication type

Start with the scientific uncertainty.

### If the question is:

> Is the original result reliable?

Prefer:

```text
Direct / close replication
```

### If the question is:

> Does the theoretical relationship survive another measurement?

Prefer:

```text
Conceptual replication
```

### If the question is:

> Does it work in another population?

Prefer:

```text
Cross-population replication
```

### If the question is:

> Does it survive laboratory variation?

Prefer:

```text
Multi-site replication
```

### If the question is:

> Does it work outside the laboratory?

Prefer:

```text
Cross-context replication
```

### If the question is:

> Does it generalise to another dataset?

Prefer:

```text
Cross-dataset replication
```

---

# 24. Combining replication types

A single study can have multiple characteristics.

For example:

```text
Independent
+
Multi-site
+
Cross-population
+
Conceptual
```

Do not force a study into only one category.

Describe the combination.

Example:

> A multi-site, cross-population conceptual replication of the original language-processing finding.

---

# 25. Avoid misleading labels

Do not call a study:

> Direct replication

if it changes:

- Population
- Intervention
- Primary outcome
- Core task
- Primary hypothesis

substantially.

Likewise, do not call a study:

> Conceptual replication

simply because the software or laboratory changed.

Minor implementation changes do not make a study conceptual.

---

# 26. Replication type versus research question

The replication type should follow the question.

Bad workflow:

```text
Choose:
"Conceptual replication"

Then:
Invent a question
```

Better:

```text
Scientific uncertainty
      ↓
Replication question
      ↓
Required design
      ↓
Replication type
```

---

# 27. Example: working-memory training

Original:

```text
Adaptive working-memory training
        ↓
Improves untrained attention
        ↓
Healthy university students
```

Possible replication designs:

### Direct

New university students, same training and outcome.

### Multi-site

New participants across multiple universities.

### Cross-population

Older adults instead of university students.

### Conceptual

Different validated working-memory training paradigm and different validated attention measure.

### Extended

Replicate the original population and additionally test durability at 6 months.

Each tests a different uncertainty.

---

# 28. Example: speech neuroscience

Original claim:

> Neural representations of speech meaning can be decoded from brain activity during naturalistic language processing.

Possible replication designs:

### Direct

Same MEG paradigm, new participants.

### Close

Equivalent MEG paradigm at an independent laboratory.

### Cross-method

MEG → OPM-MEG.

### Cross-context

One narrative → independent narratives.

### Cross-population

Healthy adults → bilingual participants.

### Conceptual

Different speech comprehension paradigm testing semantic representation.

These should not be treated as equivalent tests.

---

# 29. Example: machine learning

Original claim:

> A structural-MRI model can predict cognitive impairment.

Possible replications:

### Cross-dataset

Independent MRI cohort.

### Multi-site

Several imaging centres.

### Cross-population

Different demographic population.

### Cross-scanner

Different MRI scanners.

### Conceptual

Different model architecture using the same scientific prediction target.

The strongest design depends on what uncertainty matters.

---

# 30. Final classification checklist

Before assigning a replication type, ask:

```text
[ ] What exact scientific claim is being tested?
[ ] Is the evidence new?
[ ] Which components are essential?
[ ] Which components have changed?
[ ] Is the population the same?
[ ] Is the site the same?
[ ] Is the method the same?
[ ] Is the measurement the same?
[ ] Is the context the same?
[ ] Is the dataset independent?
[ ] Is the underlying theoretical claim unchanged?
[ ] Is the goal robustness or generalisation?
[ ] Could the design be described by multiple replication types?
```

---

# Final principle

The best replication label is the one that tells the reader **what uncertainty the new study is designed to resolve**.

```text
Original finding
      ↓
What are we uncertain about?
      ↓
Reliability?
Generalisation?
Population?
Context?
Method?
Dataset?
Theory?
      ↓
Choose replication design
```

> **Replication type should follow the scientific question, not the other way around.**
