# Scientific Communication for Research Posters

## Purpose

A research poster is a form of scientific communication designed for rapid comprehension.

Unlike a research paper, which can develop an argument over many pages, a poster must communicate the central research story in a limited visual space.

The challenge is therefore not simply to shorten the paper.

It is to identify and communicate the **most important scientific information**.

---

## 1. Communication Before Completeness

A paper asks the reader to invest time.

A poster competes for attention.

The poster should therefore prioritise:

```text id="4u9q7m"
Clarity
  ↓
Relevance
  ↓
Evidence
  ↓
Detail
```

This does not mean sacrificing scientific rigour.

It means removing information that does not contribute to understanding the research.

---

## 2. The One-Sentence Test

Before designing the poster, write the central message in one sentence.

For example:

> Speech complexity was positively associated with age in typically developing children.

This sentence becomes a constraint on the poster.

The title, figures, results, and conclusion should collectively support it.

If the central message cannot be expressed clearly, the research story may need to be clarified before visual design begins.

---

## 3. The Research Story

A strong poster usually has one dominant narrative.

A useful structure is:

```text id="b7j5rx"
Problem
  ↓
Gap
  ↓
Question
  ↓
Approach
  ↓
Evidence
  ↓
Finding
  ↓
Meaning
```

This structure should be visible in the poster layout.

The viewer should not have to reconstruct the research story from disconnected sections.

---

## 4. The Research Question as an Anchor

The research question connects the poster components.

For example:

> How does speech complexity change with age?

Then:

**Methods** explain how the question was tested.

**Results** answer the question empirically.

**Discussion** explains what the answer means.

**Conclusion** provides the final answer.

This creates structural consistency:

```text id="x8x9h1"
Question
   ↓
Method
   ↓
Result
   ↓
Interpretation
   ↓
Conclusion
```

---

## 5. Claim → Evidence → Interpretation

A useful communication framework is:

```text id="9q7j4a"
CLAIM
  ↓
What are you saying?
  ↓
EVIDENCE
  ↓
What supports it?
  ↓
INTERPRETATION
  ↓
What does it mean?
```

For example:

### Claim

> Older children produced more complex speech.

### Evidence

> Speech complexity was positively associated with age.

### Interpretation

> This pattern is consistent with increasing linguistic complexity during development.

The three should not be confused.

---

## 6. Evidence Should Be Visible

Important claims should have visible supporting evidence.

If the poster says:

> Group A performed better than Group B.

the viewer should be able to find:

- The relevant figure
- The relevant statistic
- The sample size
- The comparison

Do not hide the evidence in a small paragraph at the bottom of the poster.

---

## 7. Main Finding vs Supporting Findings

Not every finding deserves equal emphasis.

Classify findings as:

### Primary

Directly answers the research question.

### Secondary

Provides additional information.

### Exploratory

Investigates an unexpected or hypothesis-generating pattern.

The poster should visually prioritise the primary finding.

---

## 8. The 5-Second Message

Imagine a viewer walking past the poster.

After approximately five seconds, they should be able to answer:

> What is this research about?

The title and visual composition should make this possible.

If they cannot identify the topic, the poster's first-level communication has failed.

---

## 9. The 30-Second Message

After approximately thirty seconds, the viewer should understand:

- The research question
- The general method
- The main finding
- The main conclusion

This requires strong section headings and a clear visual flow.

---

## 10. The 3-Minute Message

A viewer who is interested should be able to inspect:

- Study design
- Data
- Analysis
- Figures
- Statistical evidence
- Limitations
- References

This creates three layers of communication:

```text id="1z5fkm"
5 seconds
→ Topic + main message

30 seconds
→ Question + method + result + conclusion

3 minutes
→ Evidence + details + limitations
```

---

## 11. Writing for Scanning

Poster text should be easy to scan.

Use:

- Short headings
- Short paragraphs
- Bullets
- Bold key terms
- Numbered steps
- Compact callouts
- Figures

Avoid requiring the viewer to read every sentence in sequence.

---

## 12. Headings Should Communicate Meaning

Weak:

> Results

Stronger:

> Speech Complexity Increased With Age

The second heading already communicates the finding.

Similarly:

Weak:

> Methods

Stronger:

> Narrative Language Samples Were Analysed Across Ages 4–9

Meaningful headings allow readers to scan the poster quickly.

---

## 13. Active Language

Prefer direct language.

Instead of:

> An analysis of speech complexity was conducted.

Use:

> We analysed speech complexity.

Instead of:

> A positive association was observed between age and speech complexity.

Use:

> Speech complexity was positively associated with age.

Scientific passive voice is sometimes useful, but unnecessary passive constructions often make poster text longer.

---

## 14. Remove Redundant Phrases

Poster space is valuable.

Remove phrases such as:

- "It is important to note that..."
- "The results of the analysis showed that..."
- "In the present study..."
- "It can be seen that..."
- "As can be observed from Figure 1..."

Replace them with direct statements.

For example:

> Figure 1 shows a positive relationship between age and speech complexity.

can become:

> Speech complexity increased with age (Figure 1).

---

## 15. Explain Technical Terms

A poster may be viewed by researchers outside the immediate speciality.

When technical terminology is necessary, provide a short explanation.

For example:

> **Drift rate:** the rate at which evidence accumulates toward a decision.

This is particularly useful for interdisciplinary neuroscience and computational research.

---

## 16. Audience

Before writing the poster, identify the likely audience.

Possible audiences include:

- Specialist researchers
- Researchers from related disciplines
- Students
- General academic audiences
- Conference attendees
- Clinical audiences

The level of technical detail should match the audience.

A computational neuroscience poster may need to explain a model that would be obvious to a computational modeller but unfamiliar to a cognitive neuroscientist.

---

## 17. Avoid Unnecessary Jargon

Jargon is not automatically bad.

It becomes a problem when:

- It is unnecessary
- It is undefined
- It prevents interdisciplinary readers from understanding the finding

Prefer a familiar term when it is equally precise.

If a specialised term is necessary, define it briefly.

---

## 18. Quantitative Communication

Numbers should be meaningful.

Instead of:

> β = 0.421739, p = 0.031827

consider:

> **β = 0.42, 95% CI [0.31, 0.53], p = .03**

Use an appropriate level of precision.

Do not report more decimal places than the measurement or analysis justifies.

---

## 19. Effect Size Over Significance Alone

A poster should not communicate:

> **p < .05**

as though this alone demonstrates an important scientific finding.

Where appropriate, provide:

- Effect size
- Confidence interval
- Sample size
- Relevant uncertainty

For example:

> **β = 0.42 [95% CI: 0.31–0.53]**

This gives the viewer information about the magnitude and precision of the effect.

---

## 20. Statistical Significance vs Scientific Importance

A statistically significant result is not automatically scientifically important.

Conversely, a non-significant result may still be scientifically informative.

Poster wording should reflect this distinction.

Avoid:

> Significant discovery!

Prefer:

> The analysis identified a positive association between age and speech complexity.

---

## 21. Causal Language

Be especially careful with causal language.

### Association

> Age was associated with speech complexity.

### Prediction

> Age predicted speech complexity.

### Causal interpretation

> Increasing age caused speech complexity to increase.

These statements make progressively stronger claims.

Use the strongest wording justified by the study design.

---

## 22. Exploratory Findings

Clearly label exploratory findings.

For example:

> **Exploratory analysis:** A possible relationship was observed between vocabulary size and speech complexity.

Do not visually present exploratory findings as though they were the primary hypothesis.

---

## 23. Negative Findings

Negative or null findings can be important.

A poster should not hide results simply because they are less visually impressive.

For example:

> No reliable difference was observed between conditions.

This can be scientifically valuable if it addresses the research question.

---

## 24. Limitations as Communication

Limitations should explain how the evidence should be interpreted.

Weak:

> Small sample.

Better:

> The small sample reduces precision and may limit generalisability.

Best when space allows:

> The relatively small sample may reduce the precision of the estimated effect and limit generalisation to other populations.

The goal is not to undermine the study.

The goal is to accurately communicate its evidential boundaries.

---

## 25. Conclusion Language

The conclusion should not introduce new evidence.

It should synthesise the evidence already presented.

A useful structure is:

```text id="5j0w8d"
Finding
+
Evidence boundary
+
Implication
```

For example:

> Speech complexity was positively associated with age, consistent with increasing linguistic complexity during childhood. Because the analysis was observational, the result does not establish a causal effect of age.

---

## 26. Avoiding Overclaiming

A common poster problem is making the headline stronger than the evidence.

For example:

**Evidence:**

A cross-sectional correlation between age and speech complexity.

**Overclaim:**

> Age drives language development.

**Better:**

> Speech complexity increases across age groups.

The visual prominence of the claim should also match its evidential strength.

---

## 27. Literature Communication

A poster does not need a long literature review.

Instead, use literature to establish:

1. What is known.
2. What is unknown.
3. Why the current research matters.

For example:

```text id="q7v2nm"
Previous research
      ↓
Known relationship
      ↓
Unresolved question
      ↓
Current study
```

Citations should support claims rather than decorate the poster.

---

## 28. Connecting Figures to Claims

Every important figure should have a corresponding scientific statement.

For example:

> **Figure:** Scatter plot showing age and speech complexity.

> **Claim:** Speech complexity increased with age.

The viewer should not need to infer the intended interpretation from the figure alone.

---

## 29. Avoiding Visual Miscommunication

Visual design can accidentally exaggerate evidence.

Be careful with:

- Truncated axes
- Unequal scales
- 3D charts
- Excessive smoothing
- Selective data points
- Misleading colour scales
- Enlarged differences
- Missing uncertainty

The figure should represent the evidence faithfully.

---

## 30. Poster as Conversation Starter

A conference poster often has another purpose:

**starting a scientific conversation.**

The poster should make it easy for someone to ask:

- Why did you choose this method?
- What does this result mean?
- Could this be tested longitudinally?
- How does this compare with previous work?
- Could the model generalise to another dataset?

A good poster provides enough information to support discussion without attempting to answer every possible question.

---

## 31. Designing for Questions

Include information that helps answer likely questions.

For example, a computational neuroscience poster may benefit from clearly showing:

- Dataset size
- Model architecture
- Baseline
- Evaluation method
- Main performance metric

A neuroimaging poster may need:

- Sample size
- Acquisition method
- Preprocessing
- Statistical correction
- Spatial interpretation

The poster should anticipate reasonable scientific questions.

---

## 32. Final Communication Checklist

### Message

- [ ] Can the research topic be understood in five seconds?
- [ ] Is the research question obvious?
- [ ] Is the main finding clear?
- [ ] Is the conclusion memorable?

### Evidence

- [ ] Does every major claim have supporting evidence?
- [ ] Are important quantitative results visible?
- [ ] Are figures faithful to the data?
- [ ] Is uncertainty communicated?

### Language

- [ ] Are sentences concise?
- [ ] Is unnecessary jargon removed?
- [ ] Are technical terms explained?
- [ ] Is causal language justified?

### Audience

- [ ] Is the level of detail appropriate?
- [ ] Can researchers outside the immediate speciality understand the main idea?
- [ ] Is enough methodological detail available for scientific discussion?

### Integrity

- [ ] Are exploratory findings labelled?
- [ ] Are null findings reported where relevant?
- [ ] Are limitations visible?
- [ ] Does visual emphasis match evidential strength?

---

## Core Principle

**A research poster should make the evidence easier to understand, not make the evidence look stronger than it is.**

The best poster is not the one with the most impressive design.

It is the one where the viewer can quickly understand:

**What was asked → how it was tested → what was found → what can legitimately be concluded.**
