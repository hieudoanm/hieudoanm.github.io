Does Sleep Duration Predict Cognitive Performance?

H. Doan · Cognitive Neuroscience · University of Birmingham

Background

Sleep is associated with multiple aspects of cognition, including attention, memory, and executive function. Chronic sleep deprivation has been linked to impaired cognitive performance, reduced emotional regulation, and increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases. However, the relationship between habitual sleep duration and cognitive performance in healthy adults remains debated.

Theoretical frameworks suggest that sleep supports memory consolidation through neural replay and synaptic pruning. Adequate sleep may also facilitate optimal functioning of attentional networks and executive control systems. These mechanisms predict a positive association between sleep duration and cognitive performance.

Research question

Is habitual sleep duration associated with cognitive performance?

Dataset

Behavioural and self-reported sleep measures were collected from 420 adult participants (age 18-65, mean 34.2 years). Participants completed a comprehensive cognitive battery assessing processing speed, working memory, episodic memory, and executive function. Sleep duration was assessed via the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and supplemented with 7-day sleep diaries.

The sample included diverse occupational backgrounds and educational levels. Exclusion criteria included diagnosed sleep disorders, shift work, and use of medications affecting sleep or cognition. This approach aimed to capture naturalistic variation in sleep patterns while minimising confounding factors.

Analysis

  1. Inspect distributions of sleep duration and cognitive scores for outliers and normality.
  2. Estimate the sleep–performance association using robust linear regression controlling for age, education, and physical activity.
  3. Assess uncertainty using permutation analysis (10,000 permutations) to generate non-parametric confidence intervals.
  4. Test for nonlinear relationships using restricted cubic splines.
  5. Perform sensitivity analyses excluding participants with extreme sleep durations (<5h or >10h).

Main finding

r = 0.28

Longer reported sleep duration was associated with better cognitive performance across multiple cognitive domains.

The association remained significant after controlling for age, education, and physical activity (β = 0.22, p < 0.001). Permutation analysis confirmed robustness (95% CI: 0.15-0.29). No evidence of nonlinear effects was detected, suggesting a linear relationship across the observed sleep range (5-10 hours).

Interpretation

The observed association is consistent with a relationship between sleep duration and cognitive performance. The magnitude of the effect (r = 0.28) is comparable to other established behavioural risk factors for cognitive performance, suggesting that sleep may be a meaningful modifiable target for cognitive health interventions.

Because the data are observational, the result does not establish that changing sleep duration would cause a change in cognition. Reverse causality is possible: individuals with better cognitive function may be more likely to maintain healthy sleep habits. Third variables such as stress, physical activity, or socioeconomic status could also explain the association.

The linear relationship across the observed range suggests that the association is not driven by extreme values at either end of the sleep distribution. This pattern is consistent with a dose-response relationship, which strengthens the case for a causal interpretation but does not prove it.

Limitations

  • Sleep duration was self-reported, subject to recall bias.
  • The study was observational; causal inference is limited.
  • Unmeasured variables may contribute to the association.
  • The sample may not represent the wider population.
  • Single time-point measurement does not capture sleep variability.
  • Cognitive assessment was brief and may not capture all domains.
  • No objective sleep measures (actigraphy, polysomnography) were used.

Takeaway

The data support an association between sleep duration and cognitive performance, but not a causal conclusion.

Next steps

  • Use objective sleep measurements.
  • Investigate longitudinal changes.
  • Control for additional behavioural factors.
  • Test specific cognitive domains separately.
