A Drift Diffusion Model Separates Evidence Accumulation From Decision Caution

H. Doan · Computational Neuroscience Research Group · University of Birmingham

Background

Reaction time and accuracy provide observable measures of decision behaviour, but they do not directly reveal the processes that generate a decision. The same reaction time can arise from different cognitive mechanisms: fast accumulation of strong evidence, slow accumulation of weak evidence, or a shift in decision caution.

Computational models can decompose behaviour into latent parameters that provide interpretable hypotheses about underlying cognitive processes. The drift diffusion model (DDM) is particularly powerful because it separates the efficiency of evidence processing from the decision policy—how much evidence one requires before committing to a response.

This distinction has practical implications. In clinical contexts, understanding whether slowed responses reflect reduced perceptual sensitivity or increased caution can inform different interventions. In applied settings, it can guide the design of decision support systems that account for individual differences in decision style.

Research question

Can a drift diffusion model explain differences in reaction time and accuracy between decision conditions?

Task

Participants completed a two-choice perceptual decision task. They responded as quickly and accurately as possible after viewing noisy visual stimuli. On each trial, a random-dot motion display was presented for 500ms, followed by a response window. The coherence of the motion signal varied across trials to manipulate evidence quality.

42 adult participants completed 600 trials each. The design included three coherence levels (5%, 15%, 25%) that spanned the range from near-chance performance to highly discriminable stimuli. This manipulation provides a strong test of whether the model can capture systematic changes in behaviour driven by variations in evidence quality.

Model parameters

v Drift rate: efficiency and direction of evidence accumulation.
a Boundary separation: amount of evidence required before deciding.
t₀ Non-decision time: encoding and motor-response components.
z Starting point: initial bias toward one decision.

Parameters were estimated using hierarchical Bayesian modelling, allowing group-level inference while accounting for individual differences. The model was fit using Stan with 4 chains and 2000 iterations per chain, with half used for warmup.

Model intuition

The drift diffusion model represents a decision as noisy evidence accumulating over time until one of two decision boundaries is reached. The model formalises the idea that the brain continuously samples information from the environment, updating a running tally of evidence for each possible response.

Three key components determine the behaviour: the drift rate (v), which reflects the speed and direction of evidence accumulation; the boundary separation (a), which sets the amount of evidence required before deciding; and the non-decision time (t₀), which captures processes outside the decision system like stimulus encoding and motor response execution.

Model comparison

The fitted model reproduced both the distribution of reaction times and the proportion of correct responses across experimental conditions. Model fit was assessed using posterior predictive checks and comparison with alternative model specifications.

Illustrative model-recovery comparison across two experimental conditions.

The full DDM with varying drift rate across coherence levels outperformed simpler models that assumed constant parameters (ΔLOOIC = 42.3, SE = 8.7). This supports the hypothesis that evidence quality drives systematic changes in decision processing.

Key result

v ↑ 42%

Higher-quality evidence was associated with faster, more accurate decisions, reflected in increased drift rate.

The modelling framework separates changes in evidence quality from changes in decision caution. Across coherence levels, drift rate increased systematically (β = 0.42, p < 0.001), while boundary separation remained stable, indicating that participants adapted evidence processing rather than decision policy.

Non-decision time showed a small but reliable decrease with coherence (β = -0.08, p = 0.02), suggesting modest improvements in stimulus encoding efficiency for clearer signals.

Key finding

Reaction time differences can be decomposed into interpretable cognitive processes rather than treated as a single behavioural measure.

Interpretation

In the model, drift rate provides an interpretable measure of how efficiently evidence is accumulated, while boundary separation captures how much evidence is required before committing to a response.

This distinction allows similar reaction-time changes to be explained by different underlying mechanisms. For example, slowed responses could reflect either reduced evidence quality (lower drift rate) or increased decision caution (higher boundary separation). The model provides a principled way to distinguish these possibilities.

The finding that drift rate varied with coherence while boundary separation remained stable suggests that participants adapted their perceptual processing rather than their decision policy. This pattern is consistent with optimal decision-making theory, which predicts that decision boundaries should remain constant when task demands are predictable.

Limitations

  • Model parameters are latent and depend on model assumptions.
  • Alternative models may provide different explanations.
  • Parameter recovery should be evaluated before interpretation.
  • Good behavioural fit does not prove the proposed mechanism.
  • The sample size (N=42) may limit generalisability.
  • Individual differences in strategy were not explicitly modelled.
  • The task uses artificial stimuli that may not reflect natural decisions.

Conclusion

Computational modelling provides a bridge between observable behaviour and hypotheses about latent cognitive processes. Separating evidence accumulation from decision caution can provide a more informative interpretation of reaction-time data.

Next steps

  • Compare competing cognitive models.
  • Test parameter recovery with simulated data.
  • Relate model parameters to neural measurements.
  • Investigate individual differences in decision strategy.
