A Drift Diffusion Model Separates Evidence Accumulation From Decision Caution

H. Doan · Computational Neuroscience Research Group · University of Birmingham

Background

Reaction time and accuracy provide observable measures of decision behaviour, but they do not directly reveal the processes that generate a decision.

Computational models can decompose behaviour into latent parameters that provide interpretable hypotheses about underlying cognitive processes.

Research question

Can a drift diffusion model explain differences in reaction time and accuracy between decision conditions?

Task

Participants completed a two-choice perceptual decision task. They responded as quickly and accurately as possible after viewing noisy visual stimuli.

Model parameters

v Drift rate: efficiency and direction of evidence accumulation.
a Boundary separation: amount of evidence required before deciding.
t₀ Non-decision time: encoding and motor-response components.
z Starting point: initial bias toward one decision.

Model intuition

The drift diffusion model represents a decision as noisy evidence accumulating over time until one of two decision boundaries is reached.

Model comparison

The fitted model reproduced both the distribution of reaction times and the proportion of correct responses across experimental conditions.

Illustrative model-recovery comparison across two experimental conditions.

Key result

v ↑

Higher-quality evidence was associated with faster, more accurate decisions.

The modelling framework separates changes in evidence quality from changes in decision caution.

Key finding

Reaction time differences can be decomposed into interpretable cognitive processes rather than treated as a single behavioural measure.

Interpretation

In the model, drift rate provides an interpretable measure of how efficiently evidence is accumulated, while boundary separation captures how much evidence is required before committing to a response.

This distinction allows similar reaction-time changes to be explained by different underlying mechanisms.

Limitations

  • Model parameters are latent and depend on model assumptions.
  • Alternative models may provide different explanations.
  • Parameter recovery should be evaluated before interpretation.
  • Good behavioural fit does not prove the proposed mechanism.

Conclusion

Computational modelling provides a bridge between observable behaviour and hypotheses about latent cognitive processes. Separating evidence accumulation from decision caution can provide a more informative interpretation of reaction-time data.

Next steps

  • Compare competing cognitive models.
  • Test parameter recovery with simulated data.
  • Relate model parameters to neural measurements.
  • Investigate individual differences in decision strategy.
