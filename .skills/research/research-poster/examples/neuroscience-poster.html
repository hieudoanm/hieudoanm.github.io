Neural Responses to Speech Reveal Hierarchical Processing of Linguistic Information

H. Doan · Computational Neuroscience Research Group · University of Birmingham

Research Poster MSc Cognitive Neuroimaging and Data Science

Background

Understanding speech requires the brain to integrate information across multiple timescales, from rapidly changing acoustic features (phonemes, syllables) to words, meaning, and broader narrative context. This hierarchical organisation poses a computational challenge: how does the neural system maintain representations at multiple linguistic levels simultaneously while processing a continuous input stream?

Neuroimaging studies suggest that linguistic information is represented across distributed neural systems rather than in a single specialised region. Temporal cortex tracks acoustic and phonetic information, while frontal and parietal regions support syntactic and semantic processing. However, the dynamics of how these representations emerge and interact during naturalistic speech comprehension remain unclear.

Neural decoding approaches offer a powerful tool for addressing this question. By training models to predict stimulus features from recorded neural activity, we can test what information is available in the neural signal and how it evolves over time. This approach provides a more direct test of representational content than traditional activation-based analyses.

Research question

Can neural activity during naturalistic speech processing be used to distinguish different levels of linguistic information?

Participants

36 healthy adults (age 19-34, mean 25.3 years, 18 female) participated in an auditory speech experiment. All participants were native English speakers with normal hearing and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all participants provided informed consent.

Neural responses were recorded using magnetoencephalography (MEG) while participants listened to 10 minutes of continuous spoken narratives (audiobook excerpts). Participants were instructed to listen naturally and were informed that they would answer comprehension questions afterward to maintain attention.

Interpretation

The results suggest that neural activity contains information related to linguistic structure that cannot be explained entirely by acoustic properties of the speech signal.

Key finding

Neural responses to natural speech contain information at multiple levels of linguistic representation.

Why it matters

  • Speech is a naturalistic stimulus for studying real-world cognition.
  • Neural decoding provides a quantitative way to test what information is represented in brain activity.
  • Separating acoustic and linguistic information can clarify the computational structure of speech processing.

Discussion

The additional decoding of linguistic features is consistent with hierarchical processing in which neural responses reflect information beyond the immediate acoustic signal.

However, decoding performance alone does not establish that a specific brain region causally generates a linguistic representation.

Limitations

  • Cross-sectional experimental design.
  • Limited sample size.
  • Decoding does not directly establish causal mechanisms.
  • Naturalistic stimuli introduce correlated features.

Conclusion

Neural activity during continuous speech contains information associated with both acoustic and higher-level linguistic representations. Computational decoding provides a framework for investigating how these representations evolve over time.
