# Example: Psychology Paper

A worked example of applying the `paper-reading` skill to a hypothetical psychology research paper.

The example focuses on how to read psychological research as an argument about human behaviour and mental processes.

---

# 1. Example Paper

Consider a hypothetical paper:

> **Working memory predicts individual differences in reading comprehension**

The study investigates whether individual differences in working-memory capacity are associated with differences in reading comprehension.

The basic argument is:

```text
Psychological theory
      ↓
Working memory supports language processing
      ↓
People differ in working-memory capacity
      ↓
Measure working memory
      ↓
Measure reading comprehension
      ↓
Test relationship
```

The key reading question is not simply:

> "Did working memory correlate with reading comprehension?"

Instead ask:

> **How were the psychological constructs defined and measured, what evidence supports their relationship, and what alternative explanations remain?**

---

# 2. Pass 1 — Orientation

Read:

- Title
- Abstract
- Figures
- Tables
- Headings
- Conclusion

Create an initial summary:

```text
This study examines whether individual differences
in working-memory capacity are associated with
reading comprehension ability.
```

Identify:

```text
Population:
Adults

Predictor:
Working-memory performance

Outcome:
Reading comprehension

Design:
Correlational

Main question:
Are individual differences in working memory
associated with differences in comprehension?
```

At this stage, do not assume that the study demonstrates causation.

---

# 3. Research Problem

A psychology paper often begins with a theoretical problem.

Suppose previous research suggests:

```text
Working memory is involved in language processing.
```

But researchers disagree about:

```text
How strong the relationship is
Which components of working memory matter
Whether the relationship is independent of general ability
```

The paper therefore asks whether working-memory capacity explains variation in reading comprehension.

---

# 4. Construct Before Measure

One of the most important skills in reading psychology papers is distinguishing:

```text
Construct
```

from:

```text
Measurement
```

For example:

```text
Construct:
Working-memory capacity

Measurement:
Operation span task
```

and:

```text
Construct:
Reading comprehension

Measurement:
Comprehension test score
```

The test is not the construct itself.

A score is an operational measurement intended to represent a psychological construct.

---

# 5. Operationalisation

Ask:

> How did the researchers turn an abstract psychological concept into a measurable variable?

For example:

```text
Working memory
      ↓
Operation span score

Reading comprehension
      ↓
Number of correctly answered questions
```

Then ask:

- Is the measure valid?
- Is it reliable?
- Does it measure the intended construct?
- Could performance depend on another ability?

For example, an operation-span task may involve:

- Memory
- Attention
- Processing speed
- Strategy use
- Reading ability

Therefore:

```text
Task performance
≠
Pure working-memory capacity
```

---

# 6. Hypothesis

Suppose the hypothesis is:

> Individuals with greater working-memory capacity will demonstrate better reading comprehension.

Translate it into variables:

```text
Predictor:
Working-memory score

Outcome:
Reading-comprehension score

Expected relationship:
Positive
```

A useful diagram is:

```text
Working-memory capacity
          ↓
          ?
          ↓
Reading comprehension
```

The question mark is important.

A hypothesis predicts a relationship; it does not guarantee the mechanism.

---

# 7. Participants

Suppose:

```text
N = 240 adults
Age = 18–65
```

Ask:

- How were participants recruited?
- Were they university students?
- Was participation online?
- What was the age distribution?
- What languages did participants speak?
- Were participants screened?
- Were exclusions applied?

A sample of 240 does not automatically represent the general population.

---

# 8. Sample Representativeness

Suppose participants were mostly:

```text
University students
Highly educated
Native English speakers
```

Then the findings may generalise more confidently to:

```text
Similar populations
```

than to:

```text
Children
Older adults
People with language disorders
People with different educational backgrounds
Non-native speakers
```

Always ask:

> **Who was actually studied?**

and:

> **Who is the authors' conclusion about?**

---

# 9. Experimental Design

Identify whether the study is:

```text
Experimental
Quasi-experimental
Correlational
Longitudinal
Cross-sectional
```

Suppose this study is:

```text
Cross-sectional + correlational
```

Participants complete both tasks at one time.

That means the study can establish:

```text
Association
```

more readily than:

```text
Causation
```

---

# 10. Correlation Is Not Causation

Suppose:

```text
Working memory ↔ Reading comprehension

r = 0.45
```

This indicates an association.

It does not prove:

```text
Working memory
      ↓
causes
      ↓
better reading
```

Several explanations remain possible.

### Alternative 1 — Reverse direction

```text
Reading experience
      ↓
Improved working-memory task performance
```

### Alternative 2 — Third variable

```text
General cognitive ability
      ↓
 ┌────┴────┐
 ↓         ↓
Working    Reading
memory     comprehension
```

### Alternative 3 — Measurement overlap

The two tasks may share cognitive demands.

---

# 11. Confounding Variables

Look for variables that could explain the relationship.

Potential confounders include:

```text
Age
Education
Vocabulary
Processing speed
General intelligence
Attention
Motivation
Language proficiency
Socioeconomic background
```

Suppose the raw relationship is:

```text
r = 0.45
```

but after controlling for vocabulary:

```text
partial r = 0.18
```

The interpretation changes substantially.

The relationship may partly reflect shared language ability rather than a unique relationship between working memory and comprehension.

---

# 12. Measures

Build a measurement table while reading.

| Construct             | Measure            | Possible concern               |
| --------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Working memory        | Operation span     | Also involves processing       |
| Reading comprehension | Comprehension test | Depends on vocabulary          |
| Vocabulary            | Vocabulary test    | May overlap with comprehension |
| Processing speed      | Reaction-time task | May affect multiple tasks      |

This helps separate:

```text
Psychological construct
```

from:

```text
Task performance
```

---

# 13. Reliability

Ask whether measurements are reliable.

A measurement with poor reliability can make relationships harder to detect.

Conceptually:

```text
True ability
      +
Measurement noise
      ↓
Observed score
```

If measurement noise is large:

```text
Observed relationship
```

may underestimate the underlying relationship.

Look for:

- Internal consistency
- Test-retest reliability
- Measurement error
- Reliability coefficients
- Validated instruments

---

# 14. Task Design

Read the task instructions carefully.

Ask:

- What did participants actually do?
- What was timed?
- What was rewarded?
- What counted as correct?
- Were practice trials provided?
- Were trials randomised?
- Were participants excluded for poor performance?

A task may appear to measure one ability while requiring several processes.

For example:

```text
Memory task
    +
Reading
    +
Attention
    +
Response selection
```

may produce a single score labelled:

```text
Working-memory capacity
```

That label requires interpretation.

---

# 15. Dependent and Independent Variables

Identify the variables explicitly.

For example:

```text
Independent / predictor variable:
Working-memory score

Dependent / outcome variable:
Reading-comprehension score
```

In a correlational study, however, "independent variable" can be misleading because there may be no experimentally manipulated variable.

Prefer:

```text
Predictor
Outcome
```

when appropriate.

---

# 16. Statistical Analysis

Suppose the authors use:

```text
Pearson correlation
Linear regression
```

First understand what each analysis asks.

### Correlation

```text
Are two variables associated?
```

### Regression

```text
How much does the outcome vary as a function
of the predictor(s)?
```

Then examine:

- Effect size
- Confidence interval
- p-value
- Sample size
- Model assumptions
- Residuals
- Covariates

---

# 17. Effect Size

Suppose:

```text
r = 0.45
p < .001
```

Do not stop at:

> "The result is significant."

Ask:

> **How large is the relationship?**

An effect can be statistically significant but practically small.

Conversely, an effect can be practically meaningful but statistically uncertain in a small sample.

Statistical significance and practical importance are different concepts.

---

# 18. Confidence Intervals

Suppose:

```text
r = 0.45
95% CI = [0.32, 0.56]
```

The confidence interval communicates uncertainty around the estimated relationship.

Compare that with:

```text
r = 0.45
95% CI = [-0.05, 0.70]
```

The second estimate is much less precise.

Always ask:

> How certain is the estimated effect?

---

# 19. Multiple Comparisons

Psychology studies may measure many variables.

For example:

```text
10 cognitive measures
×
5 outcome measures
```

can produce many possible statistical tests.

If researchers run enough tests, some may appear significant by chance.

Ask:

- How many analyses were planned?
- Were hypotheses preregistered?
- Were corrections applied?
- Were exploratory analyses clearly identified?
- Was the analysis plan changed after seeing the data?

---

# 20. Preregistration

Preregistration can distinguish:

```text
Confirmatory analysis
```

from:

```text
Exploratory analysis
```

For example:

```text
Before data collection:
"We predict working memory will correlate
with reading comprehension."
```

versus:

```text
After examining the data:
"We found an interesting relationship
between working memory and vocabulary."
```

Both can be scientifically useful.

The important issue is accurately representing when the hypothesis was specified.

---

# 21. Results vs Interpretation

Suppose the Results section reports:

```text
Working-memory score was positively associated
with reading-comprehension score.
```

The Discussion might say:

```text
Working memory supports the construction
of coherent mental representations during reading.
```

These are not equivalent.

The first is:

```text
Empirical result
```

The second is:

```text
Theoretical interpretation
```

The mechanism requires additional evidence.

---

# 22. Figure Reading

Suppose Figure 2 plots:

```text
X-axis:
Working-memory score

Y-axis:
Reading-comprehension score
```

with an upward regression line.

Ask:

```text
What is plotted?
What is the unit?
How variable are the observations?
Are there outliers?
How strong is the relationship?
What does the confidence band show?
Are there separate groups?
```

Do not rely only on:

> "The line slopes upward."

Look at the actual distribution of participants.

---

# 23. Group Comparisons

Suppose the paper compares:

```text
High working-memory group
vs
Low working-memory group
```

Be cautious.

Turning a continuous variable into groups can:

- Reduce information
- Depend on arbitrary cutoffs
- Reduce statistical power
- Create misleading group differences

If the original variable is continuous, ask why categorisation was necessary.

---

# 24. Mediation

Suppose the authors propose:

```text
Working memory
      ↓
Reading strategy
      ↓
Reading comprehension
```

They may perform a mediation analysis.

The important question is:

> Does the analysis establish a causal mechanism?

Usually, a cross-sectional mediation model provides evidence consistent with a proposed pathway, but does not by itself establish causal temporal ordering.

The diagram is a hypothesis about a mechanism, not proof of one.

---

# 25. Moderation

Suppose the authors test whether:

```text
Working memory → Reading comprehension
```

depends on:

```text
Age
```

This is a moderation question.

Conceptually:

```text
Age
 ↓
changes the strength of
 ↓
Working memory → Comprehension
```

Ask:

- Was the interaction tested?
- Is the interaction sufficiently powered?
- Were groups or continuous variables handled appropriately?
- Is the interaction interpreted cautiously?

---

# 26. Individual Differences

Psychology often studies variation between people.

A key question is:

> Does the observed relationship describe individuals, groups, or both?

For example:

```text
Average group difference
```

does not necessarily tell us:

```text
How an individual will behave.
```

Likewise, a correlation across individuals does not automatically describe how cognition changes within one person over time.

---

# 27. Alternative Explanations

Suppose the paper concludes:

> "Working memory is an important determinant of reading comprehension."

Generate alternatives.

### Alternative 1

```text
Vocabulary knowledge
```

may explain part of both measures.

### Alternative 2

```text
General cognitive ability
```

may influence both.

### Alternative 3

```text
Education
```

may influence both cognitive performance and reading.

### Alternative 4

```text
Task familiarity
```

may affect performance.

Critical reading asks:

> **What other explanation could produce the same pattern of results?**

---

# 28. Example Critical Assessment

Suppose the paper reports:

```text
r = 0.45
p < .001
```

and concludes:

> "Working memory is a key causal determinant of reading comprehension."

A critical reading might conclude:

```text
Evidence:
Working-memory performance is moderately associated
with reading-comprehension performance.

Strength:
The sample is reasonably large and multiple
potential covariates were measured.

Concern:
The study is cross-sectional and correlational.

Concern:
Working-memory and comprehension tasks may share
processing demands.

Concern:
Unmeasured variables such as general cognitive ability
could contribute to the association.

Conclusion:
The findings support an association between
working-memory task performance and reading comprehension,
but do not establish a causal effect of working memory
on comprehension.
```

---

# 29. Evidence Record

```text
## Research Question

Is individual variation in working-memory performance
associated with variation in reading comprehension?

## Population

240 adults aged 18–65.

## Design

Cross-sectional correlational study.

## Predictor

Working-memory task score.

## Outcome

Reading-comprehension score.

## Main Result

Working-memory performance was positively associated
with reading comprehension.

## Statistical Evidence

r = 0.45, with a reported statistically significant
association.

## Interpretation

Working memory may contribute to individual differences
in reading comprehension.

## Alternative Explanations

Vocabulary, general cognitive ability, processing speed,
education, and shared task demands may contribute.

## Limitation

The correlational design cannot establish causation.

## Contribution

The study provides evidence about the relationship
between individual differences in working-memory
performance and reading comprehension.

## Open Questions

- Does working memory causally affect comprehension?
- Which component of working memory matters?
- Does the relationship differ across age?
- Does it generalise to children?
- Does it differ in people with language disorders?
- Would experimentally increasing working-memory capacity
  improve comprehension?
```

---

# 30. Psychology-Specific Checklist

When reading a psychology paper, ask:

```text
□ What psychological construct is being studied?
□ How is the construct operationalised?
□ Is the measure valid?
□ Is the measure reliable?
□ What exactly did participants do?
□ Who participated?
□ How representative is the sample?
□ Is the design experimental or correlational?
□ What are the predictor and outcome variables?
□ What potential confounds exist?
□ Are alternative explanations addressed?
□ What is the effect size?
□ What is the confidence interval?
□ Are the statistical assumptions reasonable?
□ Were multiple comparisons considered?
□ Were analyses preregistered?
□ Which analyses are confirmatory?
□ Which are exploratory?
□ Are results separated from interpretations?
□ Does the conclusion go beyond the evidence?
□ Can the findings generalise to other populations?
□ Does the study establish association or causation?
```

---

# 31. Final Mental Model

For psychology papers, reconstruct:

```text
CONSTRUCT
    ↓
What psychological concept is being studied?

OPERATIONALISATION
    ↓
How was it measured?

SAMPLE
    ↓
Who was studied?

DESIGN
    ↓
How was the evidence generated?

MEASUREMENT
    ↓
How good are the measures?

ANALYSIS
    ↓
What relationship or effect was tested?

RESULT
    ↓
What was actually observed?

ALTERNATIVES
    ↓
What else could explain the result?

INTERPRETATION
    ↓
What psychological explanation is proposed?

GENERALISATION
    ↓
To whom does the finding apply?

CAUSALITY
    ↓
What can the design actually establish?
```

The central lesson is:

> **In psychology, carefully separate the psychological construct from the task used to measure it, the observed statistical relationship from the theoretical explanation, and association from causation.**
