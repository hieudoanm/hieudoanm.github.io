# Literature Review

A literature review synthesizes a body of research to answer a defined
question, establish what is known, evaluate the strength of the evidence,
identify disagreements and limitations, and identify gaps that motivate
future research.

A literature review is not a collection of paper summaries.

## Core Principle
> Do not summarize papers independently. Synthesize evidence across papers.

The goal is to understand the **research landscape**, not simply describe
individual studies.

## Workflow
Follow this workflow unless the user explicitly requests a narrower task:

1. Define the research question.
2. Define the scope and inclusion criteria.
3. Develop a search strategy.
4. Find relevant literature.
5. Screen studies for relevance.
6. Extract comparable evidence.
7. Assess methodological quality.
8. Group studies by themes, methods, populations, or findings.
9. Compare and contrast findings.
10. Identify areas of consensus.
11. Identify disagreements and competing explanations.
12. Identify limitations and sources of uncertainty.
13. Identify gaps in the literature.
14. Construct a coherent synthesis.
15. State conclusions proportional to the evidence.
16. Identify useful directions for future research.

## Research Question
Before searching, determine:

- What is the main research question?
- What population or system is being studied?
- What phenomenon or variables are relevant?
- What methods are relevant?
- What time period matters?
- What types of evidence should be included?
- What should be excluded?

If the question is too broad, narrow it before conducting the review.

## Search Strategy
Use multiple search terms and synonyms where appropriate.

Consider:

- Core concepts
- Synonyms
- Alternative terminology
- Abbreviations
- Related constructs
- Measurement terms
- Methodological terms
- Population terms

Prefer primary research when evaluating specific empirical claims.

Use reviews and meta-analyses to understand the field and discover
additional relevant literature, but do not treat a review as a substitute
for examining important primary studies.

## Evidence Extraction
For each important study, extract comparable information.

At minimum:

| Field             | Description                              |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Citation          | Authors and year                         |
| Research question | What was investigated?                   |
| Population        | Who or what was studied?                 |
| Sample            | Sample size and relevant characteristics |
| Method            | Experimental or analytical approach      |
| Measures          | Variables and measurements               |
| Main finding      | Primary result                           |
| Limitations       | Important methodological limitations     |
| Relevance         | Relationship to the review question      |

Add domain-specific fields when necessary.

## Synthesis
Do not structure the review as:

> Paper A found X.  
> Paper B found Y.  
> Paper C found Z.

Instead, synthesize:

> Across studies, evidence generally supports X, although studies using
> method A tend to report stronger effects than studies using method B.

Organize evidence around meaningful patterns such as:

- Research themes
- Theoretical explanations
- Methodological approaches
- Populations
- Experimental paradigms
- Findings
- Competing hypotheses
- Historical development

## Compare and Contrast
Explicitly examine differences between studies.

Ask:

- Do the findings agree?
- If not, why might they differ?
- Are the populations different?
- Are the measurements different?
- Are the experimental designs different?
- Are sample sizes different?
- Are statistical methods different?
- Could publication bias explain differences?
- Could methodological quality explain differences?
- Are the studies testing the same underlying construct?

Do not interpret conflicting results as simple contradiction without
examining methodological differences.

## Evidence Hierarchy
Consider the strength of evidence.

Distinguish between:

- Theoretical proposals
- Observational evidence
- Correlational evidence
- Experimental evidence
- Replication studies
- Longitudinal evidence
- Meta-analyses
- Systematic reviews

Do not automatically assume that a newer or more complex study provides
stronger evidence.
