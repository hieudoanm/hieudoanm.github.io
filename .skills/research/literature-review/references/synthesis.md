# Literature Synthesis

Synthesis is the central task of a literature review.

The objective is not to repeat what individual papers reported. The
objective is to combine evidence across studies into a coherent
understanding of the research question.

## Summary vs Synthesis
### Summary

> Smith et al. found X.

> Jones et al. found Y.

> Lee et al. found Z.

This describes papers independently.

### Synthesis

> Across studies, evidence generally supports X, although the effect
> appears stronger in younger participants and weaker in studies using
> self-report measures.

This identifies a pattern across studies.

## Unit of Synthesis
The basic unit should usually be a **finding, theme, method, or
theoretical claim**, rather than an individual paper.

Ask:

> What do several studies collectively tell us?

## Build an Evidence Matrix
Before writing the synthesis, organize important studies.

| Study | Population | Method | Main finding | Limitation           | Relevance |
| ----- | ---------- | ------ | ------------ | -------------------- | --------- |
| A     | Adults     | fMRI   | X            | Small sample         | High      |
| B     | Adults     | EEG    | X            | Different measure    | High      |
| C     | Children   | fMRI   | Y            | Different population | Medium    |

The matrix makes similarities and differences easier to identify.

## Compare Findings
When studies disagree, investigate the source of the disagreement.

Ask:

- Are they studying the same population?
- Are they measuring the same construct?
- Are they using comparable tasks?
- Are the stimuli comparable?
- Are sample sizes sufficient?
- Are statistical models different?
- Are effect sizes comparable?
- Are there differences in preprocessing?
- Are there differences in measurement reliability?

Do not assume that disagreement means one study is wrong.

## Consensus
A consensus should be supported by convergence across evidence.

Useful language:

- "Evidence consistently suggests..."
- "Several studies converge on..."
- "The literature generally supports..."
- "There is growing evidence that..."

Avoid:

- "It is proven that..."
- "Scientists agree that..."
- "Research definitively shows..."

unless the evidence genuinely justifies such certainty.

## Mixed Evidence
When evidence is inconsistent, explain the inconsistency.

Weak:

> Studies have produced mixed results.

Better:

> Findings are inconsistent across studies, with stronger effects
> generally reported in studies using X compared with studies using Y.
> Differences in measurement and sample characteristics may account
> for part of this variation.

## Evidence Strength
Distinguish:

```text
Observation
    ↓
Association
    ↓
Prediction
    ↓
Experimental evidence
    ↓
Causal inference
```

These are not interchangeable.

A variable predicting another variable does not automatically establish
causation.

## Avoid Citation Dumping
Do not write:

> X is important (Smith, 2019; Jones, 2020; Lee, 2021; Wang, 2022;
> Brown, 2023).

Instead, explain what the cited studies collectively contribute.

## Research Gaps
A synthesis should naturally lead to gaps.

Look for:

- Consistent findings that lack mechanistic explanation.
- Important populations that have not been studied.
- Conflicting findings without adequate explanation.
- Methods that have not been compared.
- Theories that make different predictions.
- Questions studied only indirectly.
- Poorly validated measurements.
- Lack of replication.
- Underused datasets or computational approaches.

A gap should emerge from the evidence, not be invented after the review.

## Final Synthesis
End by moving from individual findings to the state of knowledge:

```text
Individual findings
       ↓
Patterns
       ↓
Consensus
       ↓
Disagreement
       ↓
Limitations
       ↓
Uncertainty
       ↓
Research gap
```

The final synthesis should answer:

> Given the evidence reviewed, what can we reasonably conclude?
