# Critical Appraisal

Critical appraisal evaluates how trustworthy and informative a study is.

The goal is not to find reasons to reject a paper.

The goal is to determine:

> What can this study actually tell us?

## Core Questions
For every important study, consider:

1. What question did the researchers ask?
2. Was the study design appropriate?
3. Were the measurements valid?
4. Was the sample appropriate?
5. Were the analyses appropriate?
6. Are the conclusions supported by the data?
7. What limitations affect interpretation?
8. How does the study compare with other evidence?

## Research Question
Determine whether the study clearly defines what it is investigating.

Ask:

- Is the question specific?
- Is it testable?
- Are the hypotheses clear?
- Do the methods actually address the question?

A well-designed study can still provide weak evidence if it asks the
wrong question for the research objective.

## Study Design
Match the design to the claim.

| Design                      | Useful for                             |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Cross-sectional             | Associations at one point in time      |
| Longitudinal                | Change over time                       |
| Experimental                | Testing causal effects                 |
| Randomized controlled trial | Strong causal inference                |
| Case-control                | Comparing groups based on outcomes     |
| Cohort                      | Following populations over time        |
| Computational modelling     | Testing formal mechanisms              |
| Qualitative                 | Understanding experiences and meanings |

Do not infer causal conclusions from designs that cannot support them.

## Measurement
Determine whether the variables were measured appropriately.

Consider:

- Reliability.
- Validity.
- Sensitivity.
- Specificity.
- Measurement error.
- Operational definitions.
- Construct validity.

For neuroscience, also consider the properties and limitations of the
measurement technique.

For example, neural activity measured with fMRI, EEG, and MEG represents
different aspects of brain physiology and has different spatial and
temporal properties.

## Statistical Analysis
Ask:

- Is the statistical method appropriate?
- Are model assumptions reasonable?
- Was the sample size adequate?
- Were multiple comparisons addressed?
- Were effect sizes reported?
- Were confidence intervals reported?
- Was uncertainty represented?
- Was the analysis specified before examining the data?
- Were missing data handled appropriately?

Do not judge a study solely by whether its p-value is below 0.05.

## Effect Size
Statistical significance does not tell us how large an effect is.

Consider:

- Effect size.
- Confidence interval.
- Practical significance.
- Clinical or scientific relevance.

A small effect can be statistically significant in a very large sample.

A meaningful effect can fail to reach conventional significance in a small
sample.

## Multiple Comparisons
When researchers test many hypotheses, the probability of obtaining
apparently significant results by chance increases.

Consider whether the study used appropriate correction or modelling
strategies.

In neuroimaging this is particularly important because thousands of
voxels, vertices, sensors, or time points may be examined.

## Internal Validity
Internal validity asks:

> Is the observed result plausibly caused by the mechanism or relationship
> proposed by the study?

Threats include:

- Confounding.
- Selection bias.
- Measurement error.
- Attrition.
- Poor randomization.
- Experimental demand effects.

## Researcher Degrees of Freedom
Be alert to analytical choices that could influence results.

Examples:

- Exclusion criteria.
- Preprocessing choices.
- Time windows.
- Regions of interest.
- Statistical thresholds.
- Model specifications.
- Outcome selection.

A large number of reasonable analytical choices can increase the risk
of obtaining results that appear stronger than they are.

## Critical Appraisal Language
Prefer precise language.

Instead of:

> This study is bad.

Use:

> The study provides evidence for X, but the small and highly selected
> sample limits generalizability.

Instead of:

> The authors proved X.

Use:

> The findings are consistent with X, although the observational design
> does not establish causality.

## Final Principle
Critical appraisal should strengthen synthesis.

Do not simply list limitations at the end of a review.

Explain how methodological differences affect the interpretation of the
overall evidence.
