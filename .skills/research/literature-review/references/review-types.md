# Types of Literature Review

Different review types answer different research questions.

Do not treat every literature review as a systematic review.

## 1. Narrative Review

A narrative review provides a broad, interpretive synthesis of research
on a topic.

### Best for

- Introducing a field.
- Understanding theoretical development.
- Connecting findings across different methodologies.
- Identifying major themes and debates.

### Characteristics

- Flexible search strategy.
- Usually broad scope.
- Qualitative synthesis.
- Strong emphasis on interpretation and context.

### Limitation

Because the search and selection process can be less structured,
narrative reviews may be more vulnerable to selection bias.

---

## 2. Systematic Review

A systematic review uses a predefined and transparent methodology to
identify, select, evaluate, and synthesize relevant studies.

### Best for

- Focused research questions.
- Questions where comprehensive evidence identification is important.
- Evaluating the consistency of findings.

### Characteristics

- Explicit research question.
- Defined inclusion and exclusion criteria.
- Structured search strategy.
- Documented screening process.
- Formal quality assessment.
- Reproducible methodology.

### Important principle

A systematic review should be reproducible enough that another researcher
could understand how the evidence was identified and selected.

---

## 3. Scoping Review

A scoping review maps the breadth and characteristics of research on a
topic.

### Best for

- Broad or emerging research areas.
- Identifying types of available evidence.
- Mapping concepts and methodologies.
- Determining whether a more focused systematic review is appropriate.

### Typical questions

> What research exists on this topic?

> What methods have researchers used?

> Which populations have been studied?

> Where are the evidence gaps?

A scoping review generally emphasizes mapping the literature rather than
estimating a single overall effect.

---

## 4. Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis statistically combines quantitative results from
multiple studies.

It is usually performed as part of a systematic review, although
meta-analysis and systematic review are conceptually different.

### Best for

- Estimating an overall effect.
- Quantifying heterogeneity.
- Testing whether findings are consistent across studies.

A simplified fixed-effect model can be represented as:

$$
\hat{\theta}
=
\frac{\sum_i w_i\theta_i}
{\sum_i w_i}
$$

where:

- $\theta_i$ is the estimated effect from study $i$.
- $w_i$ is the weight assigned to study $i$.
- $\hat{\theta}$ is the combined estimate.

Real meta-analysis requires considerably more statistical detail,
including uncertainty, heterogeneity, model choice, and dependence
between estimates.

---

## 5. Integrative Review

An integrative review combines evidence from different research
methodologies.

For example, it might synthesize:

- Quantitative studies.
- Qualitative studies.
- Theoretical papers.
- Computational studies.

### Best for

Complex questions that cannot be adequately answered using a single
research methodology.

---

## 6. Umbrella Review

An umbrella review synthesizes existing systematic reviews and
meta-analyses rather than primarily reviewing individual studies.

It is useful when a research area already contains many systematic
reviews.

### Key challenge

The reviewer must evaluate the quality and overlap of the underlying
reviews rather than treating every review as independent evidence.

---

## Choosing a Review Type

Use the research question to determine the review type.

```text
Broad field mapping
        ↓
Scoping Review

Focused evidence question
        ↓
Systematic Review

Quantitative effect estimation
        ↓
Systematic Review + Meta-analysis

Broad theoretical understanding
        ↓
Narrative Review

Multiple evidence types
        ↓
Integrative Review

Many existing systematic reviews
        ↓
Umbrella Review
```

These categories can overlap.

A review should be described according to its actual methodology rather
than simply choosing the label that sounds most rigorous.

## Common Mistakes

Do not:

- Call a narrative review "systematic" without a systematic methodology.
- Call a collection of papers a meta-analysis.
- Treat a scoping review as an effect-estimation method.
- Assume systematic reviews automatically provide high-quality evidence.
- Ignore the quality of the studies included in a review.
- Treat review methodology as a substitute for critical appraisal.
