## Selection, not amplification

More information reaches the senses per second than the brain can process — on
the order of ten million bits of sensory input against a processing budget of
tens of bits per second. Attention is the selection mechanism that closes that
gap, and everything downstream depends on the choices it makes.

The word _selective_ carries the theory. It is not that unattended input is
blocked at the door; it is processed to some depth, without awareness. That is
why a shadow moving in your peripheral vision still grabs you, and why a
conversation you are not in is not so much inaudible as hard to follow.

Two properties of attention are easier to feel than to measure. It is
**limited** — performance degrades as demands rise — and it is **selective and
non-uniform** in the sensory world, concentrated in space, time, and feature.

## The kinds of attention

The standard divisions describe _where_ attention points and _what_ it is for.

**Selective** attention filters one channel from several: your name spoken
across a noisy room, the cocktail party effect. **Sustained** attention is
vigilance over time — a radar operator's job, and one where performance decays
within twenty minutes without rest. **Alternating** attention switches between
tasks, and every switch carries a cost, the _attentional blink_, during which
the second of two stimuli in quick succession is often missed entirely.

**Divided** attention runs two channels at once. It is genuinely possible, but
the cost shows up as error and delay rather than as a clean split. People are
poor judges of this: studies of driving while texting find performance dropping
substantially while participants rate their own driving as safe and unchanged.
The subjective estimate and the objective cost come apart, which is why divided
attention failures persist despite being obvious in retrospect.

**Executive** attention is the supervisory one — holding a goal, inhibiting what
competes with it, and switching when the goal changes. It maps onto prefrontal
cortex, and it is the component that dissociates most cleanly from raw capacity
after brain injury: vocabulary and general knowledge survive, planning and
inhibition do not.

## Unattended processing still happens

Attention is not a gate, and treating it as one is the most common mistake.

**Inattentional blindness** describes missing a stimulus that is fully visible
but not attended — a gorilla in a basketball video. The classic variant swaps
the images during a blink and most viewers report no change at all, which is
**change blindness**: the stimulus is gone, and awareness of its absence never
formed.

Processing that requires no attention is real and measurable. Your name
presented in another stream captures awareness without your choosing it, and it
works for words you have never consciously registered — the name need not be on
your list of exceptional categories at all.

The practical implication is that **unattended does not mean unprocessed**. What
survives unattended processing is determined by the properties of the stimulus,
not by the intentions of the person, which is why a stimulus designed to be
salient — high contrast, unexpected, personally relevant — reaches awareness far
more reliably than one that merely occurs.

## Expectations shape what gets through

Attention is not a neutral aperture. It is tuned by expectation, and the tuning
is measurable in the timing of responses to surprising stimuli.

Two consequences follow. **Top-down guidance** means you find what you were
looking for — which is efficient and, at the same time, why you miss what you
were not. **Change blindness** follows: an object not in the attentional set can
be swapped for a near-identical one without detection. And because attention is
where perception, memory, and reasoning meet, it is not surprising that
performance on any of them drops when a competing task is added — the same
narrow gate serves all three.

## Divided attention and the cost of switching

Task switching is not free. The _switch cost_ is the extra time taken on the
first action after a change, typically a few hundred milliseconds, and it is
larger the more similar the two tasks are because the mental set has to be
unbuilt as well as rebuilt.

Switching also disrupts unfinished work: a switch mid-stream increases the time
to return to the original task, and the time grows with how long the original
task was held. The practical reading is that frequent interruptions cost more
than their duration suggests, and that batching is a measurable advantage rather
than a stylistic preference.

## Deliberate practice of attention

Attention is trainable in the ordinary sense of getting better at a task with
feedback, but the training is narrow. Meditation and breath-focus practice
improve sustained attention measures and the ability to notice mind-wandering,
with effect sizes that are real and modest — they do not reliably transfer to
other tasks, and claims of broad "attention improvement" outrun the evidence.

What does transfer is reducing the cost of the situations that break attention:
fewer competing channels, one task at a time, and external cues that mark the
transition between tasks so you do not have to hold the intention yourself.
