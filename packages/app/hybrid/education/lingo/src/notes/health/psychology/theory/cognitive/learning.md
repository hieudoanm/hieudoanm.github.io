## What counts as learning

Learning is a relatively durable change in behaviour or knowledge that comes
from experience rather than maturation, fatigue, or a drug. Three qualifications
do a lot of work in that sentence.

_Durable_ rules out the effects of a single dose or a single bad hour.
_Experience_ rules out maturation — an infant walking at one year has not
learned to walk. And _behaviour or knowledge_ leaves room for learning you
cannot demonstrate until the right test arrives, which is the trap that produced
two of the field's most famous results.

## Classical conditioning

In Pavlov's arrangement a neutral bell is paired with food until the bell alone
elicits salivation. Three properties define the learned response.

**Acquisition** is the pairing phase, and its speed depends on how predictably
the neutral stimulus signals the important one. A signal that arrives
unpredictably — sometimes with food, sometimes not — is a weak conditioner,
because it carries little information.

**Extinction** is what happens when the pairing stops: the conditioned response
fades. Extinction is not erasure, though. After a rest, the response reappears
in weakened form — _spontaneous recovery_ — and it can be **renewed** by
returning the animal to the context where conditioning happened. The original
association is still there; extinction is new learning laid over it.

That gives the practical warning: you can train a fear response out of someone
and have it return intact the first time they are back in the room. Exposure
works; exposure and _never returning to the room_ does not.

The contingency matters more than the pairing. **Blocking** shows it: establish
tone-to-light, then present tone-with-light together. The light gets no
conditioning, because the tone already predicts food and nothing was left to
learn. **Higher-order conditioning** shows that learned signals can signal
learned signals, which is how phobias generalise across categories that never
were directly paired with harm.

## Operant conditioning

In operant learning a behaviour is shaped by its consequences. Four quadrants
follow from whether the consequence follows or precedes the behaviour and
whether the consequence adds or subtracts something.

**Positive reinforcement** adds a stimulus after a behaviour, raising its rate.
**Negative reinforcement** removes one, also raising the rate. **Positive
punishment** adds an aversive stimulus, lowering it. **Negative punishment**
removes a valued one, lowering it too. Most everyday descriptions get this
backwards: _reinforcement_ and _punishment_ name the change in behaviour;
_positive_ and _negative_ name whether something was added or taken away.

Punishment is the weak half for a structural reason. A punisher teaches what not
to do without teaching what to do instead, so behaviour tends to be suppressed
rather than replaced — and suppression generalises poorly, showing up as
avoidance, aggression, or behaviour that shifts somewhere unsupervised.
Reinforcement shapes behaviour toward something. Where you can reinforce the
alternative, do that instead.

The **schedule** matters as much as the consequence. Behaviour learned on a
**continuous** reinforcement schedule extinguishes fastest once reinforcement
stops, because every absence is informative. Behaviour learned on a
**variable-ratio** schedule — reinforcement after an unpredictable number of
responses — resists extinction almost completely. Gambling machines, and a great
deal of compulsive checking, are built on it deliberately.

## Learning without reinforcement

Tolman's latent learning result is the reason behaviourism is not the last word.
Rats ran a maze daily with no reward and showed no sign of learning it. The
moment a reward appeared at the far end, they ran it directly. The learning had
happened; it had simply never been expressed, because nothing in the environment
asked for it.

This separates **acquisition** from **performance**: knowing something and
showing what you know are different processes, and observation, instruction, and
exploration all produce learning that only shows up when something makes showing
it worthwhile.

**Observational learning** extends the point to other people. Bandura's Bobo
dolls established that children copy modelled behaviour they have never been
reinforced for, and that attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation are
the four processes in between. What gets copied is not the whole behaviour but
the _outcome_ it produced — people reproduce behaviour that was rewarded in
front of them, not merely demonstrated.

## Cognitive accounts

Cognitively oriented theories treat learning as the acquisition and
restructuring of representations rather than the stamping in of stimulus–
response links.

Latent learning is the cleanest evidence. **Expectancy learning** adds the
subjective side: Tolman's rats learned the outcome that followed, not only the
motor route, which is why they kept trying after the reward was removed.

**Reinforcement learning** in the modern, computational sense makes the
consequences explicit as values attached to states, and predicts behaviour from
prediction error rather than from satisfaction. It explains exploration through
the exploration bonus, and it explains why choices shift when the value of an
option is recomputed rather than when a rule is applied.

**Rule learning** describes what people actually do when learning a domain
conventionally: induct a compact structure — a rule, a grammar, an algorithm —
that captures the regularities of many examples, then apply it to new ones. Rule
learning explains why expertise looks like rapid abstraction rather than
accumulated specifics, and why transfer to structurally similar problems is
common while transfer between superficially similar problems is often nil.

## Learning is expensive and slow

The gap between demonstration and behaviour is the practical problem. Learning a
new motor skill or a new rule set takes far more trials than performance
eventually requires, and most of the cost is paid in errors during exactly the
phase where the learner believes they are competent.

Two things reliably improve the rate. **Feedback that arrives per attempt and is
specific** about what to change — not a score at the end, but a cue to the
action. And **spreading practice across sessions**, which is the same spacing
effect that governs retention of facts and works about as well for skills.
