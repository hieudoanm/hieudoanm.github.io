## Depression is a syndrome, not sadness

Clinical depression — major depressive disorder — is a defined syndrome, not
ordinary low mood. The DSM-5 criteria require at least five of nine symptoms
present most of the day, nearly every day, for **two weeks or more**, and at
least one of them must be depressed mood or loss of interest and pleasure.

The remaining symptoms span sleep, appetite and weight, energy, concentration,
psychomotor speed, and feelings of worthlessness or guilt, with recurrent
thoughts of death. The pattern must cause distress or impair function and must
not be better explained by a substance, a medication or another medical
condition.

Grief is not the same thing. Bereavement comes in waves and leaves self-esteem
intact; a major depressive episode is more persistent and pervasive and usually
brings self-criticism and hopelessness with it.

## How common and how disabling

The WHO estimates that depression affects roughly **280 million people**
worldwide and is a leading cause of disability, not death. Lifetime risk is on
the order of 15–20% in high-income countries, with women diagnosed about twice
as often as men.

Most episodes resolve, but recurrence is common: around half of people who
recover will have another episode, and the risk rises with each one. Despite
effective treatments, a large share of cases go unrecognised or untreated — the
WHO calls this the treatment gap.

## Screening is not diagnosis

Two instruments dominate routine screening. The **PHQ-9** maps the nine DSM
criteria onto a frequency scale and gives a severity total, with a validated
cut-off of 10 for probable major depression. The **BDI-II** asks about 21
cognitive, affective and somatic symptoms over the past two weeks.

Both have good sensitivity and specificity, and both are used to **track**
severity over time. Neither diagnoses depression. A positive screen means the
question is worth asking, not that the answer is known — diagnosis still takes a
clinical interview that weighs duration, impairment and alternative causes.

## What the evidence supports

For mild presentations, guidelines favour active monitoring and structured
psychological therapy over medication. For moderate to severe episodes, the
strongest evidence is for cognitive behavioural therapy, behavioural activation
and interpersonal therapy, and for antidepressants, with combination treatment
often outperforming either alone.

Regular aerobic exercise has a small but reliable effect and supports sleep and
mood. Sleep and circadian disruption both predict and worsen episodes, so
stabilising routine is part of treatment rather than an optional extra.

## Risk and getting help

Thoughts of death or self-harm are a medical emergency, not a detail to monitor.
Anyone in crisis should contact local emergency services or a crisis line in
their country right away, and should not wait for a scheduled appointment.

This page is educational. It explains what the instruments measure and what the
evidence says; it is not a diagnosis and not a substitute for assessment by a
qualified clinician.
