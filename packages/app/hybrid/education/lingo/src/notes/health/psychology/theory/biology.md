## Neurons and their electricity

A neuron maintains a resting potential of roughly **−70 mV** across its
membrane, held there by the sodium–potassium pump and potassium leak channels. A
stimulus that crosses threshold depolarises the cell, and voltage-gated sodium
channels open in a regenerative **all-or-none** spike.

Because the spike is all-or-none, intensity cannot be encoded by spike size. It
is encoded by **frequency** and by **how many cells** respond — a principle
called population coding. Stronger stimulus, faster firing.

At the synapse, most neurotransmitters cross a **chemical** gap, adding a delay
of milliseconds. The exception is electrical synapses, which are faster and
rarer.

## Where signals go

Sensory pathways relay information from receptors toward the cortex, and motor
pathways run the other way. The longest of these, the **corpus callosum**, links
the two hemispheres across some 200 million axons.

Not all cortex does one job. The visual cortex in the occipital lobe, the motor
cortex in the frontal lobe, and the **hippocampus** in the medial temporal lobe
are anatomically separate, and separating them — as in the patient H.M. —
separates their functions too.

The thalamus acts as a relay hub, gating which signals reach awareness, while
the cerebellum tunes timing and coordination on a millisecond scale.

## Learning changes the wiring

Experience alters synaptic strength. At the molecular level, **long-term
potentiation** (LTP) — persistent strengthening after repeated co-activation —
inserts extra AMPA receptors into the postsynaptic membrane, so the same input
later produces a larger response.

Hebb summarised it: neurons that fire together wire together. This is how a
short-lived experience becomes a durable skill or memory, and it is why sleep,
rest and spacing out study sessions matter — consolidation is biological work,
not a metaphor.

## The body shapes the mind

Brain and body are one system, not two. The **hypothalamus** drives the
autonomic nervous system and the endocrine system, and endocrine hormones feed
straight back into the brain.

That feedback runs in both directions. Stress releases cortisol and adrenaline,
which sharpens short-term memory and impairs retrieval; regular aerobic exercise
increases BDNF and supports neuroplasticity. The vagus nerve carries roughly 80%
of autonomic afferent traffic, so the body reports constantly — and the brain
listens.
