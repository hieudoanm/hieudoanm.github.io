## Anxiety is a signal, not a disorder

Anxiety is a normal alarm. It narrows attention, primes the body for action and
helps performance up to a point — the inverted-U every student feels before an
exam. It becomes a disorder only when the response is out of proportion to the
threat, persists after the threat has gone, or starts to dictate what a person
can do.

The anxiety disorders share that core but differ in their trigger: generalised
anxiety disorder (GAD) attaches to many everyday concerns, panic disorder to
sudden surges of fear, social anxiety to scrutiny by others, and phobias to
specific objects or situations. Obsessive-compulsive and trauma-related
conditions sit nearby but are classified separately.

## When worry becomes generalised

GAD is defined less by any single fear than by **uncontrollable worry** across
several domains — work, health, money, family — on more days than not for at
least **six months**. Alongside it come restlessness, being easily fatigued,
poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension and disturbed sleep.

The worry is not useful problem-solving. It is repetitive, hard to stop and
often about things the person cannot control, so it consumes time and attention
without resolving anything. Many people with GAD have lived with it so long that
the tension feels like personality rather than a condition.

## How common and how disabling

The WHO estimates that anxiety disorders affect roughly **300 million people**
worldwide, and they are among the most common mental-health conditions. GAD
specifically has a lifetime prevalence of around 5–6%, with women affected about
twice as often as men, and an onset that is usually in early adulthood.

They are also costly. Anxiety disorders commonly co-occur with depression, and
the combination drives more impairment than either alone. The WHO counts them
among the leading contributors to years lived with disability, and, as with
depression, the majority of people affected never receive treatment.

## Screening is not diagnosis

The **GAD-7** is the standard brief screen. Seven items ask how often, over the
past two weeks, a person has been bothered by nervousness, uncontrollable worry
and related symptoms, each scored 0–3 for a total of 21. A cut-off of 10 gives
sensitivity around 89% and specificity around 82% for GAD.

The GAD-7 tracks severity over time and is short enough for routine use, but it
does **not** diagnose. A high score shows the question is worth asking, not that
the answer is known; diagnosis still needs a clinical interview that weighs
duration, impairment, other conditions and physical causes of the symptoms.

## What the evidence supports

For mild presentations, guidelines favour active monitoring, psychoeducation and
structured psychological therapy. Cognitive behavioural therapy has the largest
evidence base, and exposure-based approaches are central for the phobic and
panic disorders; for GAD, CBT and applied relaxation are first-line.

Medication — most commonly SSRIs and SNRIs — is considered for moderate to
severe presentations or when therapy is unavailable, and combination treatment
is common. Regular exercise, sleep and reduced caffeine help manage arousal but
support treatment rather than replace it.

## Risk and getting help

Anxiety is not dangerous by itself, but severe panic can feel like a heart
attack, and anxiety that drives avoidance or withdrawal can shrink a life
quickly. Anyone whose anxiety is stopping them from working, studying or leaving
the house should seek assessment rather than wait for it to pass.

This page is educational. It explains what the instrument measures and what the
evidence says; it is not a diagnosis and not a substitute for assessment by a
qualified clinician.
