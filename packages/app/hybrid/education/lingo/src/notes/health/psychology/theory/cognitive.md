## Attention is a filter, not a spotlight

At any moment far more information reaches the senses than the brain can
process, so attention selects. Selective attention means focusing on one channel
while suppressing others — the reason a conversation you are not in becomes hard
to follow.

The **cocktail party effect** is the classic demonstration: your name spoken
across a noisy room pulls attention to you without you consciously choosing it,
and the effect survives even when the same words are played backwards.

Attention is also capacity-limited. Divided attention costs accuracy and
reaction time, which is why phone use while driving degrades performance even
though people rate it as safe.

## Working memory holds the present

Working memory is a small, temporary store — able to hold roughly four items,
not the seven Miller famously reported. Miller&apos;s magic number was about the
span of **absolute judgement** under ideal conditions; real-world chunking
usually yields closer to three or four chunks.

Baddeley&apos;s model separates it into a **central executive** that directs
attention, a **phonological loop** for speech and sound, a **visuospatial
sketchpad** for images and locations, and an **episodic buffer** that binds them
into a coherent scene.

Change one component and the pattern of errors changes. Ask people to repeat
digits and they fail on length; ask them to match columns of letters while
reciting words and they succeed at both.

## Long-term memory is many systems

Long-term memory is not one store. It divides at least three ways: by
**content** (declarative facts and episodes versus procedural skills), by
**consciousness** (explicit recall versus implicit priming), and by **stage**
(encoding, consolidation, retrieval).

The serial position curve is a reliable finding: items at the start and end of a
list are recalled best, the middle worst. The **spacing effect** — long gaps
between study attempts outperforming massed ones — is among the most robust
findings in all of memory research.

Retrieval is reconstructive, not a replay. Each act of recall slightly rewrites
the memory, which is why confident eyewitnesses can be wrong and why repeated
retelling can introduce errors.

## Language and executive control

Language processing is largely automatic and unconscious. We rarely notice the
work behind parsing a sentence until it fails — garden-path sentences such as
“the old man the boats” initially commit you to the wrong reading and then force
a reanalysis.

Executive function coordinates working memory, attention, inhibition and
planning, and keeps goals active over time. Damage to the prefrontal cortex
degrades it while leaving vocabulary and general knowledge largely intact, which
is the dissociation clearest evidence that they are separate systems.
