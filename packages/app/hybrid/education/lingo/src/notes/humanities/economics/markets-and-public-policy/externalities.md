# Externalities

> When your actions affect strangers who never asked—for better or worse.

App route: `/economics/externalities/`

## What is it?

An **externality** is a cost or benefit from a transaction that spills over to
people who are not part of the deal. A **negative externality** (like pollution
from a factory) imposes costs on others; a **positive externality** (like a
vaccinated neighbor) bestows benefits on others. Because these effects occur
outside the market price, they cause markets to produce too much of the harmful
activity or too little of the beneficial one.

## Negative externalities

**Costs borne by others:** The factory’s smoke harms nearby residents, but the
factory doesn’t pay for that damage—so it treats the cost as if it doesn’t
exist.

**Market failure:** The private cost to the producer is lower than the true
social cost. The result is overproduction: too much pollution, congestion, and
resource depletion relative to what society would want.

**Examples:** Factory emissions, traffic congestion, secondhand smoke, and loud
noise are all familiar negative externalities.

## Positive externalities

**Benefits to others:** When benefits spill over without the producer being
compensated, the market under-provides the activity—too little of a good thing.

**Examples:** Education makes society more productive, vaccines protect the
unvaccinated, R&D benefits other firms, and well-maintained gardens raise
neighborhood property values.

**The gap:** Because individuals reap only part of the benefit, they invest too
little. This is why public education, immunization campaigns, and research
funding are subsidized.

## The solutions

**Pigouvian taxes:** Arthur Pigou proposed taxing activities with negative
externalities so producers pay the true social cost. Carbon taxes are the modern
example—they make polluters internalize the harm.

**Subsidies:** Paying for positive externalities (education grants, vaccine
subsidies, research tax credits) pushes provision up toward the socially optimal
level.

**Coase theorem:** Ronald Coase showed that if property rights are clear and
bargaining is costless, parties can negotiate to the efficient outcome without
government—though in reality transaction costs usually prevent this.

**Regulation and cap-and-trade:** Command-and-control rules or tradable permits
directly limit harmful activity while letting the market allocate who reduces
emissions.
