## What is it?

**Moral hazard** occurs when one party changes their behavior after a deal is
made because they do not bear the full consequences of their actions. The
classic setting is insurance: once insured, a person takes fewer precautions
because the cost of loss falls largely on the insurer. It arises whenever
someone is shielded from risk.

## How it works

**Before the deal:** An uninsured homeowner installs a security system and
checks for fire hazards. The full cost of loss is theirs, so they take care.

**After the deal:** Once fully insured, the incentive to maintain security
weakens. The insurer bears most of the loss—so the homeowner is less careful.
That shift in behavior is moral hazard.

**Asymmetric information:** The insured party knows their own behavior; the
insurer cannot observe it directly. This information gap is what makes moral
hazard hard to eliminate.

## Real-world examples

**Health insurance:** Insured patients may visit doctors more often or choose
costlier treatments because they pay only a copay—the insurer covers the rest.

**Bank bailouts:** Banks that expect to be rescued by the government take larger
risks. The implicit guarantee shifts potential losses to taxpayers.

**Corporate expense accounts:** Employees spending company money have less
incentive to economize than when spending their own.

## The solutions

**Deductibles and copays:** Making the insured party pay part of every claim
keeps skin in the game and reduces careless behavior.

**Monitoring and audits:** Insurers and employers observe behavior—driving
telematics, expense receipts—to catch and deter excessive risk-taking.

**Contractual incentives:** Performance bonuses, clawback clauses, and aligned
compensation structures reduce the gap between private and social costs.

**Screening and selection:** Offering different policy tiers forces agents to
self-select, revealing their true risk level.
