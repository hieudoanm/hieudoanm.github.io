# Oligopoly & Strategic Competition

> A few big firms, each watching the others—markets where strategy decides
> everything.

App route: `/economics/oligopoly/`

## What is it?

An **oligopoly** is a market dominated by a few large firms whose decisions are
interdependent—for example, airlines, telecoms, and auto manufacturers. Because
each firm’s best move depends on what rivals do, oligopoly is the natural home
of game theory. Firms can compete on quantity (Cournot), compete on price
(Bertrand), or collude for monopoly-style profits.

## The core models

**Cournot competition:** Firms choose output simultaneously. Each anticipates
rivals’ production and picks its own. The equilibrium sits between perfect
competition and monopoly—fewer firms than ideal, but more output than a
monopolist.

**Bertrand competition:** Firms choose price instead of quantity. If products
are identical, even two firms can push price down to competitive cost—cutting
prices to steal the whole market. Outcomes differ sharply from Cournot.

**Game-theoretic thinking:** In either model, each firm’s profit depends on
rivals’ choices, so solving the market means finding a Nash equilibrium of the
strategic game.

## Collusion and cartels

**Why collusion tempts:** If firms agree to keep output low and prices high,
they can split near-monopoly profits instead of competing them away.

**Why it’s fragile:** Each firm has an incentive to secretly cut price or expand
output to win the others’ business—exactly the logic of the Prisoner’s Dilemma.
Trust is hard to sustain.

**Repeated games:** When the market repeats over and over, cooperation can
survive because cheaters face retaliation in future rounds—tit-for-tat dynamics
make collusion more stable.

**Illegality:** Explicit cartels are illegal in most countries, but firms often
find tacit ways to coordinate, such as price leadership.

## Why it matters

**Market outcomes between extremes:** Oligopoly usually yields higher prices and
lower output than perfect competition, but better outcomes than monopoly—the
exact result depends on the rules of the game.

**Antitrust enforcement:** Regulators scrutinize mergers among the few big
players and punish price-fixing, because collusion among oligopolists harms
consumers.

**Strategic decisions:** Advertising, capacity, product launches, and pricing in
airlines and telecoms are all played as games—firms anticipate rival reactions
in every move.

**Measuring market power:** Concentration indices like the Herfindahl-Hirschman
Index help regulators gauge how close a market is to oligopoly.
