## What is it?

**Signaling** is the theory of how people convey hidden qualities to others.
Michael Spence (Nobel Prize 2001) showed that a signal only works if it is
**costly to fake**— otherwise everyone could send it and it would convey
nothing. The classic example is education as a signal of ability: getting a
degree is costly, so it credibly separates high-ability from low-ability
workers.

## What makes a credible signal

**Costly to fake:** A signal must be harder or more expensive to produce for
people who don’t possess the hidden quality. If everyone could fake it, it would
carry no information.

**Differentiating cost:** The signal works when it is easier (cheaper) for the
“good” type to produce than the “bad” type—so the good type invests in it and
the bad type rationally declines.

**Observable:** A signal must be visible to the other party for it to influence
their behavior.

## Real-world examples

**Education:** A degree signals perseverance, intelligence, and trainability.
Even if it teaches little, its scarcity and cost make it a credible signal of
ability in the job market.

**Brands and warranties:** A money-back guarantee signals product quality
because a poor product would be costly for the firm to back—low-quality firms
can’t profitably offer it.

**Certifications:** Professional licenses and industry certifications (CPA, CFA)
signal competence because they require costly effort and testing.

**Animal and human display:** The peacock’s tail and a firm handshake both
signal fitness because they are costly to produce—evolutionary signaling.

## Signaling vs screening

**Signaling:** The informed party moves first, voluntarily revealing their type
through a costly action—the job applicant invests in a degree.

**Screening:** The uninformed party moves first, designing a mechanism that
sorts participants—the employer sets a test or a credential requirement that
candidates self-select against.

**Why it matters:** Together these solve the adverse selection and
hidden-information problems that can otherwise collapse markets (the market for
lemons). Understanding which is happening tells us who bears the effort of
overcoming the information gap.
