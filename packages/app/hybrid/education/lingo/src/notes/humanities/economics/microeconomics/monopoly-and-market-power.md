## What is it?

A **monopoly** exists when a single seller dominates a market with no close
substitutes. Because it faces no competition, the monopolist holds **market
power**: the ability to raise price above the competitive level and still keep
customers. The result is usually higher prices, lower output, and a transfer of
wealth from consumers to the firm.

## Sources of monopoly

**Barriers to entry:** Monopolies persist because new competitors cannot easily
enter. High fixed costs, control of key inputs, and strong economies of scale
keep rivals out.

**Natural monopoly:** When one firm can serve the whole market at lower average
cost than two or more firms—as with water, electricity, and rail—competition is
wasteful and a single provider is natural.

**Legal and strategic:** Patents, licenses, and exclusive rights grant temporary
monopolies, while anti-competitive practices and network effects can entrench
dominant firms.

## How a monopolist prices

**Price setter, not taker:** Unlike a competitive firm that accepts the market
price, a monopolist chooses a price by balancing higher price against fewer
sales.

**Marginal revenue < price:** To sell more, the monopolist must cut the price on
all units, so the extra revenue from an additional sale is less than the price
charged. This drags output below the competitive level and price above it.

**Profit-maximizing quantity:** The monopolist produces where marginal revenue
equals marginal cost, then charges the highest price that quantity will bear.

## The cost to society

**Deadweight loss:** Because the monopolist charges above marginal cost, some
buyers who value the product more than its cost don’t buy. Those foregone
transactions are pure waste—a loss to society with no corresponding gain.

**Reduced innovation:** Without competitive pressure, a protected monopolist has
weaker incentives to improve products or cut costs—though the promise of
monopoly profits also funds research.

**Rent seeking:** Firms may spend resources lobbying to obtain or protect a
monopoly position, diverting effort from productive activity.

**Antitrust response:** Competition policy—breaking up monopolies, blocking
anti-competitive mergers, and regulating natural monopolies—aims to restore
competition and protect consumers.
