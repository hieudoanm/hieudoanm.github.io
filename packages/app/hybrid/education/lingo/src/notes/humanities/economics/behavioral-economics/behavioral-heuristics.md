# Behavioral Heuristics & Biases

> The mental shortcuts that usually work—but sometimes lead us badly astray.

App route: `/economics/behavioral-heuristics/`

## What is it?

**Heuristics** are the fast, automatic mental shortcuts people use to make
decisions under uncertainty. They evolved because they usually work quickly and
well—but they also produce systematic **cognitive biases**: predictable errors.
Resources developed by Kahneman and Tversky distinguish the fast **System 1**
thinking (intuitive, effortless) from the slow **System 2** (deliberate,
effortful).

## Common heuristics

**Availability:** People judge how likely something is by how easily examples
come to mind. Dramatic news makes rare risks (plane crashes) seem common, while
common risks (car crashes) are underestimated.

**Representativeness:** People judge probability by how similar something is to
a stereotype, ignoring base rates. This fuels the gambler’s fallacy and
stereotype-based errors.

**Anchoring:** Initial numbers pull judgments toward them—the first price seen
anchors what seems reasonable, even when it is arbitrary.

**Confirmation bias:** People seek and remember evidence that supports what they
already believe while ignoring the rest.

## Why it matters

**Systematic, not random:** Biases are patterned rather than chaos, so
economists and psychologists can study and predict them—and design interventions
to counteract them.

**Against perfect rationality:** These findings challenge the classical economic
assumption that people are fully rational, leading to behavioral economics (see
also Prospect Theory and Nudge).

**Real stakes:** Biases distort saving, investing, medical decisions, and jury
verdicts. Awareness alone doesn’t eliminate them, but design and structure can.

## Countering biases

**Slow down:** Engaging System 2—pausing, checking assumptions, and seeking
disconfirming evidence—reduces many impulsive errors.

**Use decision aids:** Checklists, algorithms, and statistical reasoning tools
override unreliable intuitions in medicine, finance, and hiring.

**Debias the environment:** Governments and firms can adjust defaults, reframe
information, and present statistical context to help people choose better
(nudges).

**Aggregate diverse views:** Decision teams with diverse perspectives and
structured dissent expose individual blind spots.
