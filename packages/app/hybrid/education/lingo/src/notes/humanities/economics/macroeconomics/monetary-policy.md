## What is it?

**Monetary policy** is how a central bank manages the money supply and interest
rates to achieve its goals—typically price stability, full employment, and
moderate growth. By raising or lowering rates and buying or selling assets, it
influences how much borrowing, spending, and investment happen across the
economy. The overarching goal is to keep inflation low and stable so that money
holds its value.

## The tools

**Interest rates:** The policy rate is the central lever. Higher rates make
borrowing dearer, cooling spending and inflation; lower rates stimulate
borrowing and demand.

**Open market operations:** Buying government bonds injects money into the
economy; selling them drains it.

**Quantitative easing:** In deep downturns, central banks buy longer-term assets
to push down long-term rates when policy rates are already near zero.

**Reserve requirements and guidance:** Regulations and explicit statements about
future policy shape bank lending and market expectations.

## Why inflation matters

**Money losing value:** Inflation erodes purchasing power, hurts savers, and
distorts prices and contracts.

**Expectations are central:** If people expect high inflation, they raise prices
and wages, making high inflation self-fulfilling. This is why central banks work
hard to anchor expectations—credibility is the key asset (see Time
Inconsistency).

**The quantity theory:** In the long run, sustained inflation is driven by money
growing faster than output. Central banks limit it by controlling money growth.

**Deflation is also bad:** Falling prices can discourage spending and force
debts to become harder to repay, which central banks hence target a small
positive inflation rate.

## Modern practice

**Inflation targeting:** Most major central banks commit to an explicit target
(often ~2%), anchoring expectations and guiding policy decisions.

**Independence:** Central banks operate free of short-term political control,
which makes their anti-inflation promises credible.

**Data-driven decisions:** Policymakers weigh employment, growth, and inflation,
adjusting policy gradually and communicating clearly to shape market
expectations.

**Limits:** Monetary policy is less effective when rates are at zero, when
inflation expectations are unanchored, or when the problem is supply-side rather
than a demand shortfall.
