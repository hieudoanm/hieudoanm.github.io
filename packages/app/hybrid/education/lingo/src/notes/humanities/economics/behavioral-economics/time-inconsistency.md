## What is it?

**Time inconsistency** occurs when a person’s or institution’s preferences now
differ from the preferences they will hold later. A classic example is monetary
policy: a central bank wants low inflation, but once people expect low
inflation, the bank is tempted to create surprise inflation to boost output.
Finland’s Kydland and Prescott won the Nobel Prize in 2004 for showing why such
rules beat discretion, and why **credibility** matters.

## Present bias

**The problem of now vs later:** People discount the near future much more
steeply than the distant future—a pattern called hyperbolic discounting. “Start
the diet Monday” feels easy, but when Monday arrives, the salad again loses to
the cake.

**Planning vs action:** The same person can genuinely prefer saving for
retirement and simultaneously spend impulsively. Preferences are not stable over
time, which is why good intentions fail.

**Commitment devices:** Anticipating future weakness, people voluntarily
restrict their future choices—savings accounts with withdrawal penalties, gym
contracts, or deleting distracting apps. Rational today protecting against
irrational tomorrow.

## Policy credibility

**The inflation bias:** If the central bank merely announces low inflation,
private agents rationally anticipate the temptation to inflate and build in
higher wage demands. The result is higher inflation with no extra output—the
worst of both worlds.

**Rules over discretion:** Binding rules (like a fixed inflation target or a
monetary rule) remove the temptation, producing better outcomes than letting
policymakers decide each period.

**Central bank independence:** Giving central banks independence and clear
mandates makes them more credible. Because they no longer face electoral
pressure, their promises are believed—lowering inflation expectations without
sacrificing policy flexibility.

## Real-world examples

**Dieting and exercise:** People buy gym memberships and prepay for meal plans
precisely because they cannot trust their future selves to choose well in the
moment.

**Retirement saving:** Default auto-enrollment exploits present bias—people stay
in plans rather than actively opting out.

**Peace negotiations:** Governments promise to demobilize, but each side doubts
the other will follow through. Third-party guarantees and enforcement make these
commitments credible.

**Carbon commitments:** Countries announce climate targets knowing future
governments may not honor them—the credibility problem at global scale.
