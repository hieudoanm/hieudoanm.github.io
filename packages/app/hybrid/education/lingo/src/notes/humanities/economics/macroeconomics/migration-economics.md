## What is it?

**Migration economics** studies the causes and consequences of people moving
across borders. At its core, migration is a **human capital investment**:
individuals move when the expected gains—higher wages, better
opportunities—outweigh the costs of moving, including adjustment and foregone
income. It is one of the largest flows in the global economy and a powerful
engine of both individual and aggregate gains.

## Why people move

**Wage differentials:** Large wage gaps between countries create powerful
incentives to relocate where labor is paid more.

**Opportunity and safety:** People migrate for jobs, education, family, and to
escape conflict, insecurity, or disaster.

**Networks:** Family and community connections in the destination lower the cost
and risk of migrating, so migration often builds on itself along established
corridors.

## The gains

**For migrants:** Earnings often rise enormously upon moving, making migration
one of the largest income gains an individual can realize.

**For receiving economies:** Migrants fill labor shortages, contribute skills
and taxes, and add to output and innovation. The long-run fiscal effect is
generally small or positive.

**For sending economies:** **Remittances** sent home support families and can
fuel development, while skills and experience gained abroad may return.

## Costs and policy

**Short-run adjustment:** In receiving areas, rapid new arrivals can put
pressure on wages and public services in specific regions or groups, though
effects are often small for the overall economy.

**Brain drain:** The departure of highly skilled workers can deprive sending
countries of talent they invested in.

**Policy design:** Balancing openness with integration—language, labor markets,
housing—determines how widely the gains from migration are shared.
