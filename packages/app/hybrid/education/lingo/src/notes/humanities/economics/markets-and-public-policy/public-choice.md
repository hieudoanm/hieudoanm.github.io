## What is it?

**Public choice** applies the methods of economics to politics. Instead of
assuming policymakers selflessly serve the public good, it treats politicians,
bureaucrats, and voters as self-interested actors responding to incentives.
Pioneered by James Buchanan (Nobel Prize 1986) and Gordon Tullock, it explains
why government can fail as surely as markets can—often called the “economics of
politics.”

## Self-interest in politics

**Politicians:** Seek re-election and power. Policy choices are shaped by what
wins votes, not just what is efficient.

**Bureaucrats:** Seek budget growth, prestige, and job security. Agencies may
pursue expansion even beyond what the public interest requires.

**Voters:** Are rationally ignorant because a single vote rarely decides an
election, so they invest little effort in becoming informed—leading to
poorly-considered outcomes.

## Government failure

**Logrolling:** Politicians trade votes to pass legislation. Each legislator’s
pet projects pass, but the accumulated package benefits a few at the expense of
many.

**Rent seeking:** Special interests spend resources lobbying for favors,
subsidies, and protection—diverting effort from productive activity to influence
the state.

**Concentrated benefits, diffuse costs:** Small groups (subsidized farmers,
protected industries) are politically powerful because they care intensely,
while the broad public each pays a little and doesn’t organize. This biases
policy toward the few.

## Constitutional design

**Rules matter:** Buchanan emphasized that the rules of the political game—the
constitution—are as important as the moves players make within them.
Well-designed rules channel self-interest toward good outcomes.

**Checks and balances:** Separating powers, requiring supermajorities, and
constraining government limit the damage that self-interested officials can do.

**Decentralization:** Federalism and local control let citizens “vote with their
feet,” forcing governments to compete and limiting abuse of power.

**Transparency:** Open government and public scrutiny raise the cost of
self-dealing and align officials’ behavior with the public interest.
