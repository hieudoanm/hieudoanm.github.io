# Overconfidence Bias

> People systematically overestimate their abilities, forecasts, and control
> over outcomes.

App route: `/economics/overconfidence-bias/`

## What is it?

**Overconfidence** is the tendency to be more certain of one’s knowledge,
judgments, and abilities than the evidence warrants. It takes several
forms—overestimating skill, over-precision in beliefs, and excessive optimism
about the future. It is one of the most robust and consequential biases in
behavioral economics.

## The three forms

**Overestimation:** Believing you are better, faster, or smarter than you
actually are—nearly everyone ranks themselves above average at driving,
leadership, and competence.

**Over-precision:** Being too sure your estimates are correct; people assign far
narrower confidence ranges than the true uncertainty warrants.

**Over-optimism:** Underestimating the likelihood of bad outcomes and
overestimating good ones—starting ventures, planning projects, expecting
success.

## Where it bites

**Trading:** Overconfident investors trade too frequently, and higher trading is
associated with lower returns (see Behavioral Finance).

**Business:** Managerial overconfidence drives overambitious projects and
deal-making—firms with overconfident CEOs invest more and overpay for
acquisitions.

**Forecasting:** Underestimating uncertainty produces overconfident predictions
of growth, earnings, and timelines, with systematically missed targets.

## Why it persists

**Self-serving bias:** People credit success to skill but blame failure on bad
luck, shielding confidence from evidence.

**Confirmation bias:** Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs while
ignoring contradictions reinforces overconfidence.

**Occasional benefits:** Some optimism motivates risk-taking and persistence
that can pay off (entrepreneurship); the cost is often borne when it distorts
judgment. Mitigating it requires explicit consideration of opposing evidence and
honest calibration of uncertainty.
