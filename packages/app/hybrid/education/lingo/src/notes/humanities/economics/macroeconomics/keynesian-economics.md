## What is it?

**Keynesian economics**, from John Maynard Keynes’s 1936 _General Theory_,
argues that aggregate demand—total spending in the economy—is the dominant
driver of output and employment in the short run. During recessions, spending
can fall short, leaving the economy stuck below full employment. Because markets
don’t automatically snap back, Keynes advocated active government intervention
to stabilize demand.

## The core problem: demand

**Sluggish adjustment:** Wages and prices are sticky in the short run, so they
don’t fall fast enough to clear markets. A drop in demand thus leads to
unemployment, not just lower prices.

**Sticky wages:** Workers resist nominal pay cuts, so firms respond to falling
demand by laying people off, which cuts spending further—a downward spiral.

**The paradox of thrift:** When everyone saves more in a downturn, aggregate
demand falls and total income falls, so overall saving may not rise.
Individually sensible, collectively harmful.

## The policy response

**Fiscal policy:** Government spending and taxation can boost demand. If the
private sector won’t spend, the state can spend instead—through infrastructure,
transfers, or tax cuts—to lift output back toward full employment.

**The multiplier:** A dollar of government spending ripples through the
economy—each recipient spends some of it again. The total boost to demand can be
a multiple of the original spending.

**Monetary policy:** Central banks can support demand by lowering interest
rates, making borrowing cheaper and spending more attractive.

**Countercyclical stance:** Governments should run deficits in recessions and
surpluses in booms—spending against the grain of the business cycle.

## Debates and legacy

**Crowding out:** Critics argue that government borrowing raises interest rates
and displaces private investment, weakening the stimulus. Keynesians reply this
matters less when the economy is far below capacity.

**Supply-side critique:** Classical economists contend that expansionary policy
mainly causes inflation and misallocation.

**The middle ground:** Most modern macroeconomics blends Keynesian short-run
demand management with supply-side realities—the neoclassical synthesis. Central
banks now routinely manage demand as part of mainstream policy.
