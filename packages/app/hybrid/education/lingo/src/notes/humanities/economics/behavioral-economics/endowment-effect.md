## What is it?

The **endowment effect** is the finding that people value something more once
they own it. Simply possessing an item raises the price they demand to part with
it above what they would have paid to acquire it in the first place. It is one
of the most robust demonstrations that preferences depend on the reference point
of ownership, contradicting standard economic assumptions.

## How it is demonstrated

**The classic mugs study:** In a famous experiment, randomly chosen students
given a coffee mug demanded far more to sell it (willingness to accept) than
non-owners offered to buy it (willingness to pay)—despite random assignment
meaning no true value difference existed.

**Reference dependence:** The gap arises because owners anchor on the loss of
the item, while buyers anchor on the gain—consistent with loss aversion.

**Robust but not universal:** The effect is strongest for goods people feel
attached to or use regularly, and weaker for money or purely exchangeable goods.

## The psychological driver

**Loss aversion:** Giving up an owned object is felt as a loss, which hurts
twice as much as an equivalent gain feels good (see Prospect Theory)—so owners
overprice what they’d sell.

**Identity and attachment:** Owned items become part of the self, making their
loss feel like a personal loss.

**Status quo bias:** The endowment effect is a special case of favoring the
current situation over a change, making people stick with what they have (see
Status Quo Bias).

## Why it matters

**Voluntary exchange puzzles:** A strong endowment effect can reduce the gains
from trade predicted by rational models, since sellers value goods more than
buyers.

**Investment behavior:** Investors become reluctant to sell assets they
own—especially losers—contributing to the disposition effect in finance.

**Policy and pricing:** Free trials and ownership framing exploit the effect;
companies that let customers “own” a product first make them value it more and
are harder to give up.
