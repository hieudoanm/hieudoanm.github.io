## What is it?

The **Tragedy of the Commons** describes a situation where individuals, acting
independently and rationally according to their own self-interest, behave
contrary to the best interests of the whole group by depleting a **shared
resource**. The classic example is herdsmen sharing a common pasture: each adds
more cattle because it benefits them personally, but the overgrazing destroys
the pasture for everyone.

## The core dilemma

**Individual incentive:** The benefit of consuming more of the resource goes
entirely to the individual, while the cost (depletion) is spread across
everyone. So each person has a rational incentive to take more, even though the
collective outcome is ruinous.

**Rivalrous and non-excludable:** Common resources are rivalrous (one person’s
use reduces what’s left for others) but non-excludable (hard to stop anyone from
using them). This combination is what creates the trap.

**The paradox:** What is rational for each individual is irrational for the
group. The resource collapses even though nobody wanted that outcome.

## Real-world examples

**Overfishing:** Each fishing boat catches more than the sustainable yield
because the fish it doesn’t take will be caught by someone else—collapsing
entire fish stocks.

**Groundwater depletion:** Farmers pump as much water as available, drawing down
aquifers faster than they can recharge.

**Climate change:** Each nation benefits from burning fossil fuels while the
cost of global warming is borne by the entire planet.

**Public land and traffic:** Overgrazing commons, congested roads during rush
hour, and crowded natural parks all follow the same logic.

## The solutions

**Property rights:** Turning the commons into privately owned resources gives
owners an incentive to manage them sustainably—you don’t deplete what you own.

**Government regulation:** Rules, quotas, and taxes (like fishing limits or
carbon pricing) constrain individual take.

**Community governance:** Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize in 2009 for showing
that communities can sustainably self-manage commons through shared rules,
monitoring, and gradual sanctions.

**Why it matters:** The tragedy is not inevitable. With the right institutions,
shared resources can be preserved indefinitely.
