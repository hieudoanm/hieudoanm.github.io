# Consumer Theory & Indifference Curves

> How rational consumers choose between goods to get the most satisfaction from
> a limited budget.

App route: `/economics/consumer-theory/`

## What is it?

**Consumer theory** models how a rational buyer chooses a combination of goods
to maximize utility, or satisfaction, subject to a budget constraint. Its visual
tool is the **indifference curve**: a set of bundles giving the same utility.
The consumer’s optimum occurs where an indifference curve just touches the
budget line—balancing preferences against affordability.

## Preferences

**Completeness:** A consumer can compare any two bundles and state which they
prefer or whether they are indifferent.

**Transitivity:** If A is preferred to B and B to C, then A is preferred to
C—consistency in ranking.

**More is better:** With scarce goods, consumers prefer more of a good to less,
giving indifference curves their downward slope.

**Diminishing marginal rate of substitution:** As a consumer has more of one
good, they give up less of another to gain a bit more, making indifference
curves convex.

## Budget and optimum

**The budget constraint:** Income limits what the consumer can afford. The
budget line shows all affordable bundles; its slope is the ratio of the two
prices.

**The optimum:** The consumer reaches the highest indifference curve that still
touches the budget line. At the optimum, the marginal rate of substitution
equals the price ratio.

**Responding to prices:** When a price changes, the budget line rotates, and the
consumer moves to a new optimum. This change splits into a **substitution
effect** (choosing relatively cheaper goods) and an **income effect** (feeling
richer or poorer).

## Why it matters

**Deriving demand:** Consumer theory explains why demand curves slope downward
and how demand responds to price and income changes—the foundation of market
analysis.

**Separating goods into types:** Normal goods, inferior goods, and substitutes
versus complements are classified by how demand reacts to income and the prices
of related goods.

**Policy design:** Understanding consumer choice helps design taxes, subsidies,
and welfare programs that anticipate how people will reshuffle their spending.

**A baseline:** Indifference-curve analysis gives an ideal-rational benchmark
against which behavioral deviations (see Prospect Theory) are measured.
