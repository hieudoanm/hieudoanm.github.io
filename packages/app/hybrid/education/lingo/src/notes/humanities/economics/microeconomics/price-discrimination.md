## What is it?

**Price discrimination** is selling the same or similar product to different
buyers at different prices. A firm can do this when it has market power, can
identify customers with different willingness to pay, and can prevent resale
between them. It lets firms capture more of the consumer surplus that would
otherwise go to customers, raising revenue and often expanding output.

## Three degrees

**First degree (perfect):** Charge each customer exactly their maximum
willingness to pay, capturing all surplus. Rare in practice, but targeted
pricing and negotiation approach it.

**Second degree (by quantity or version):** Charge different prices for
different amounts or versions—volume discounts, and premium vs. basic
tiers—letting customers self-select.

**Third degree (by group):** Charge different groups different prices—student
discounts, senior prices, or country-based pricing—when groups differ in price
sensitivity.

## Examples in practice

**Airline tickets:** The same seat sells at vastly different prices based on
booking time, flexibility, and traveler type—a classic mix of second- and
third-degree discrimination.

**Software tiers:** Free, personal, professional, and enterprise plans price
discriminate by usage and willingness to pay.

**Coupons:** Cutting coupons lets price-sensitive shoppers pay less while others
pay full price, sorting buyers by their elasticity.

**Dynamic pricing:** Algorithms adjust prices in real time based on demand,
time, and user characteristics.

## Is it good or bad?

**Captures surplus:** Firms earn more, capturing value that would otherwise
remain with consumers—a transfer, not necessarily a waste.

**Can expand output:** By lowering prices for elastic customers, discrimination
can serve people who otherwise wouldn’t buy, increasing output and possibly
shrinking deadweight loss.

**Equity concerns:** Charging groups differently can feel unfair, and aggressive
personalization raises privacy worries.

**Arbitrage limits it:** Discrimination only works if buyers can’t resell
between segments—which is why travel and services (with identity checks)
discriminate more than goods.
