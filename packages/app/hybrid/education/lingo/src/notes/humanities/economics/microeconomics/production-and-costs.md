## What is it?

**Production** is the process of turning inputs (labor, capital, materials) into
outputs (goods and services). A **production function** describes how much
output results from given inputs. Costs follow from production: firms pay for
inputs, and the structure of these costs—fixed versus variable, marginal versus
average—determines how much they produce and at what price.

## The production function

**Inputs to output:** Adding inputs generally raises output, but at a decreasing
rate—the **law of diminishing marginal returns**: each additional worker adds
less output once other inputs are fixed.

**Marginal product:** The extra output from one more unit of an input. It
typically first rises, then falls as the input is overused relative to fixed
factors.

**Returns to scale:** Scaling up all inputs together can produce proportionally
more (increasing), less (decreasing), or the same (constant) output—governing
long-run size decisions.

## Cost curves

**Fixed vs variable costs:** Fixed costs don’t change with output (rent,
equipment); variable costs do (materials, labor). Short-run decisions weigh
variable and marginal costs.

**Marginal cost:** The extra cost of producing one more unit. The
U-shape—falling then rising—mirrors diminishing returns.

**Average costs:** Average total cost falls while marginal cost is below it and
rises once marginal cost exceeds it— so marginal cost crosses average cost at
its minimum.

## Costs and supply

**Profit-maximizing output:** A firm produces up to the point where marginal
cost equals marginal revenue. In competitive markets, price equals marginal
revenue, so output is set where price equals marginal cost.

**The supply curve:** A competitive firm’s short-run supply curve is its
marginal cost curve above the shutdown point—it supplies more when price rises.

**Entry and exit:** In the long run, profits attract entry and losses trigger
exit, pushing prices toward the minimum of average total cost—the benchmark of
efficient production.
