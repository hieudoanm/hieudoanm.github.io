## What is it?

**Adverse selection** arises when one side of a transaction knows something the
other side doesn’t—before the deal is made. Because the uninformed party cannot
tell good from bad, they set a price for the average, which drives away good
participants and attracts bad ones. George Akerlof’s famous paper on the _market
for lemons_ (1970, Nobel Prize 2001) showed this can cause markets to shrink or
collapse entirely.

## The market for lemons

**Hidden quality:** A used-car seller knows whether their car is a peach or a
lemon; the buyer doesn’t. The buyer can only offer a price reflecting the
average car.

**Selective exit:** Owners of good cars refuse to sell at an average price, so
only lemon owners sell. The pool of cars in the market deteriorates toward the
bottom.

**Market failure:** In the extreme, no one trades good cars at all, and the
market for high-quality goods collapses even though people value them—a genuine
market failure from hidden information.

## Real-world examples

**Health insurance:** If insurers can’t distinguish healthy from sick customers,
they price for the average risk. Healthier people then decline coverage, leaving
a sicker, costlier pool—raising premiums further.

**Insurance in general:** Auto and life insurers face the same problem: the
people most eager to buy are often the most expensive to cover.

**Labor markets:** An employer can’t always tell productive from unproductive
applicants, so wages reflect the average—discouraging the best candidates from
applying.

**Credit markets:** Lenders who can’t distinguish good from risky borrowers
attract riskier ones.

## The solutions

**Screening:** The uninformed party gathers information to sort
participants—medical exams, credit checks, warranties, and used-car inspections
all screen out bad risks.

**Signaling:** The informed party voluntarily reveals quality through costly
signals (see the Signaling page): education, certifications, guarantees, and
brand reputations.

**Government intervention:** Mandatory insurance pools and underwriting bans
force broad participation so that risk is shared rather than selected against.

**Market mechanisms:** Third-party ratings, licensed intermediaries, and
reputation systems help buyers trust quality before they buy.
