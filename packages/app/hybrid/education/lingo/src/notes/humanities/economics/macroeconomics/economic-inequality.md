## What is it?

**Economic inequality** refers to how unevenly income and wealth are distributed
across a population. It is measured with tools like the **Gini coefficient** (0
= perfect equality, 1 = total concentration) and the share of income held by the
top 10% or 1%. Inequality shapes opportunity, social stability, and the
effectiveness of policy—and it has risen sharply in many countries since the
1980s.

## Measuring it

**Income vs wealth:** Income is the flow earned each year; wealth is the
accumulated stock of assets. Wealth inequality is usually much larger than
income inequality because wealth compounds and concentrates across generations.

**Gini coefficient:** A single number summarizing the concentration of a
distribution—derived from the Lorenz curve by comparing actual distribution to a
perfectly equal one.

**Top shares and percentiles:** Banerjee and Piketty popularized tracking the
income share of the top 1% or 0.1% to see where growth is really going.

## Causes

**Technology and skills:** Skills-biased technological change rewards the highly
educated while automating routine work, widening the pay gap between skilled and
unskilled.

**Globalization and offshoring:** Trade with low-wage countries puts downward
pressure on wages for less-skilled workers in advanced economies.

**Capital income and returns:** As economist Thomas Piketty emphasized, when the
return on capital exceeds economic growth (r > g), wealth accumulates faster
than incomes, driving inherited inequality.

**Institutions and policy:** Taxation, education access, union strength, and
inheritance laws all shape how income and wealth are distributed.

## Consequences and responses

**Opportunity:** High inequality can entrench unequal access to education,
health, and opportunity, reducing social mobility for the next generation.

**Growth and stability:** Moderate redistribution can support growth and social
stability, while extreme inequality may fuel instability and reduce aggregate
demand.

**Policy tools:** Progressive taxation, social transfers, universal education,
and inheritance taxes redistribute and equalize opportunity; the design of these
is hotly debated.

**A rising global phenomenon:** Within-country inequality is rising in many
places even as global-between-country inequality falls—both trends matter for
policy and political dynamics.
