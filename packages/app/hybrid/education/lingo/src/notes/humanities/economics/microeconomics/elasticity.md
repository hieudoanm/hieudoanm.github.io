## What is it?

**Elasticity** measures how strongly quantity demanded or supplied responds to a
change in price (or income). It answers the question “by what percentage does
quantity change when price changes by one percent?” Goods that react a lot are
**elastic**; those that barely budge are **inelastic**. Knowing elasticity tells
businesses and governments how price changes will ripple through a market.

## Price elasticity of demand

**Elastic demand:** Quantity changes more than proportionally to price. Luxury
goods, items with close substitutes, and big-ticket purchases are typically
elastic—a small hike in price sharply cuts purchases.

**Inelastic demand:** Quantity barely changes with price. Essentials like
insulin, salt, and gasoline are inelastic because buyers cannot easily switch or
go without.

**What drives it:** Availability of substitutes is the biggest factor. Fewer
substitutes, smaller budget share, and short time horizons all make demand more
inelastic.

## Elasticity and revenue

**The key business insight:** If demand is elastic, raising price lowers total
revenue (quantity falls faster than price rises). If demand is inelastic,
raising price raises revenue.

**Unit elastic:** At the point where elasticity equals one, total revenue is
maximized—further price moves in either direction reduce it.

**Strategic use:** Airlines, streaming services, and tax authorities exploit
differences in elasticity to price discriminate and optimize revenue.

## Why it matters

**Tax incidence:** Whether a tax lands on buyers or sellers depends on relative
elasticities. The more inelastic side bears more of the burden—regardless of who
legally pays it.

**Opioid and tobacco policy:** Taxes on cigarettes reduce consumption more among
price-sensitive young people, because their demand is more elastic.

**Supply elasticity:** Over time supply becomes more elastic as producers find
alternatives, which is why long-run responses often differ sharply from
short-run reactions.

**Income elasticity:** It separates necessities from luxuries—necessities have
low income elasticity, luxuries high—shaping how spending shifts as nations grow
richer.
