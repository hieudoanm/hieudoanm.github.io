# Mental Accounting

> Why people treat money differently depending on where it comes from—and where
> it’s going.

App route: `/economics/mental-accounting/`

## What is it?

**Mental accounting**, coined by Richard Thaler, describes how people mentally
categorize money into separate “accounts” based on its source and intended use,
rather than treating all money as fungible. A rational person would treat a
dollar as a dollar regardless of origin; in practice, people earmark tax
refunds, gifts, or gambling winnings as “extra” and spend them more freely than
ordinary income.

## How it shows up

**Windfall spending:** A bonus or refund is placed in a “found money” account
and splurged, while ordinary salary is guarded as “real” money.

**Budgeting by category:** People allocate fixed budgets to food, entertainment,
and bills, and resist spending across categories even when total spending would
be sensible.

**Expensive vs cheap accounts:** Borrowing on a credit card while holding idle
savings reflects keeping separate mental pools rather than netting them out.

## Why it matters

**Errors in savings:** Treating a tax refund as spending money while carrying
debt ignores that paying down debt is effectively a risk-free return.

**Rationalizing vice:** People use mental accounts to justify spending—“I earned
this treat”—even when it contradicts their own budgets.

**Investment framing:** Investors segregate gains and losses into accounts (see
the disposition effect), altering how they hold or sell assets relative to a
holistic view.

## The rational integration

**Money is fungible:** A dollar in any account has the same purchasing power, so
the rational view treats resources as one pool.

**Improved by rules:** Rather than fighting the impulse, rational people exploit
mental accounting—using separate “accounts” as a self-control device, such as
automatic savings or a dedicated travel fund.

**A double-edged tool:** Mental accounting is a bias when it distorts spending,
but a useful constraint when deliberately harnessed to enforce saving goals.
