## What is it?

**Behavioral economics** fuses psychology and economics to explain why people
systematically deviate from rational choice. A **nudge**, as defined by Richard
Thaler and Cass Sunstein (Nobel Prize 2017 for Thaler), is any aspect of choice
architecture that alters behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any
option or changing economic incentives. The default option, the order of items,
and the framing of outcomes are all nudges.

## Key concepts

**Libertarian paternalism:** It is possible to steer people toward better
decisions while preserving their freedom to choose otherwise. The architecture
is paternalistic; the outcome is libertarian.

**Default effects:** People overwhelmingly stick with whatever option is
pre-selected. Organ donation rates jump from ~15% to ~90% when the default
switches from opt-in to opt-out—even though the actual choice is identical.

**Framing effects:** How a choice is presented changes what people pick. “90%
fat-free” is more appealing than “10% fat”—same information, different response.

**Social norms:** Telling people what others do is one of the most powerful
nudges. Hotel towel reuse programs that say “75% of guests reuse their towel”
outperform environmental appeals.

## Choice architecture

**Simplification:** Reducing complexity and decision fatigue. Auto-enrollment in
retirement plans with a simple opt-out is far more effective than requiring
active sign-up.

**Salience:** Making important information conspicuous. Placing healthy food at
eye level in a cafeteria increases its selection without removing junk food.

**Timely feedback:** Showing energy usage compared to neighbors (the Opower
effect) nudges conservation without any price change.

**Commitment devices:** Helping people lock in future good behavior—like savings
apps that penalize withdrawal—addresses present bias.

## Criticisms and limits

**Manipulation concerns:** Critics argue nudges are a form of soft paternalism
that can be used to manipulate rather than help—especially when the choice
architect has its own agenda.

**Scale and durability:** Small nudges may produce small effects that fade over
time. Structural changes (taxes, regulations) often have larger, more permanent
impacts.

**Equity:** Nudges can disproportionately affect less sophisticated
decision-makers, potentially widening the gap between sophisticated and naive
consumers.

**Replication:** Some classic nudge effects have proven fragile or
context-dependent, raising questions about generalizability.
