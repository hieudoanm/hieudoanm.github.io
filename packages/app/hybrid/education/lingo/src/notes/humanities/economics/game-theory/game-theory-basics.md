## What is it?

**Game theory** is the study of strategic interaction: situations where the best
course of action for each person depends on what others do. A **game** consists
of players, their available strategies, and the payoffs that result from each
combination of choices. Game theory provides the formal language for analyzing
everything from chess and poker to pricing, auctions, and international
conflict.

## Key building blocks

**Players and strategies:** Players are the decision-makers; strategies are the
complete plans of action available to them, including contingent responses to
what others do.

**Payoffs:** The utility each player receives from every possible combination of
strategies. Rational players aim to maximize their own payoff.

**Normal vs extensive form:** Games can be described as a simultaneous payoff
matrix (normal form) or as a tree of sequential moves (extensive form), each
suited to different questions.

**Information:** Whether players know the rules, each other’s moves, and each
other’s preferences (perfect, imperfect, complete, or incomplete) deeply shapes
play.

## Solution concepts

**Dominant strategies:** A strategy that is best no matter what others do. When
one exists, predicting play is easy.

**Nash equilibrium:** A combination of strategies where no player can improve by
changing alone. The central prediction of non-cooperative game theory (see Nash
Equilibrium).

**Subgame-perfect equilibrium:** A refinement for sequential games requiring
credible strategies at every decision point (see Backward Induction).

**Mixed strategies:** Randomizing among actions when no pure strategy is safe,
such as in rock-paper-scissors.

## Why it matters

**Economics:** Game theory underpins the modern theory of oligopoly, auctions,
bargaining, and mechanism design—how firms, bidders, and institutions behave
strategically.

**Biology and evolution:** It models the spread of behaviors in populations (see
Evolutionary Game Theory).

**Computer science:** Used in adversarial AI, auctions on the internet, and the
design of protocols where participants may be self-interested.

**Social science:** Analyzes arms races, deterrence, bargaining in politics, and
the emergence of social norms.
