## What is it?

The **labor market** matches workers’ supply of effort with firms’ demand for
it, setting wages and employment. In a competitive model, a worker’s pay
reflects their marginal contribution to output. The **minimum wage** is a
legally imposed floor on wages, designed to lift the earnings of the
lowest-paid. Whether it helps or hurts depends on how firms and workers adjust—a
question with some of the most fiercely debated evidence in economics.

## How wages form

**Demand for labor:** Firms hire up to the point where an additional worker’s
contribution (marginal product) equals the wage. Higher wages or lower
productivity reduce hiring.

**Supply of labor:** Workers offer more hours when wages rise—though beyond a
point higher wages can reduce hours as people choose more leisure.

**Equilibrium wage:** The wage clears the market where the quantity of labor
supplied equals the quantity demanded. Deviations from it create unemployment or
labor shortages.

## The minimum wage debate

**The classic view:** If the floor is set above the market wage, firms demand
fewer workers and hire less—the canonical textbook prediction is some job loss,
especially among the youngest and least-skilled workers.

**The monopsony view:** When employers have market power (few firms hiring in a
town), they pay below the competitive wage. A moderate minimum wage can then
raise wages without reducing jobs—possibly even increasing employment.

**The evidence:** Empirical studies, including the famous Card & Krueger
fast-food experiment (Nobel 2021), found little employment effect from modest
minimum-wage increases. The consensus is that modest floors mostly raise pay
with small employment effects, while very high floors can cause measurable job
loss.

## Who it affects

**Low-wage workers:** Minimum wages directly raise the earnings of those at the
bottom, often reducing poverty—though many beneficiaries are teenagers and
second earners in non-poor households.

**Teenagers and part-timers:** The least-experienced workers face the highest
risk of reduced hiring when the floor rises.

**Prices and employers:** Firms may offset higher labor costs by raising prices,
cutting hours, or substituting automation, shifting part of the burden to
consumers.

**Spillovers:** Raising the floor can compress the wage ladder, nudging up pay
for workers just above the minimum and changing broader wage distributions.
