## What is it?

**Human capital** is the stock of knowledge, skills, health, and abilities that
people accumulate through education, training, and experience, which makes them
more productive. The economist Gary Becker modeled it as an investment: people
give up time and money today to build skills that pay higher earnings in the
future—much like investing in physical capital.

## Investing in people

**Education:** Formal schooling raises earnings and productivity; the return to
schooling is consistently high across countries.

**On-the-job training:** Firms and workers invest in skills through training
that boosts productivity and wages.

**Health and nutrition:** Healthier, well-nourished workers are more productive
and can work more—health is a vital form of human capital, especially in
developing economies.

## The returns

**Individual returns:** More education and skill are strongly associated with
higher lifetime earnings—one of the most reliable regularities in economics.

**Growth returns:** A better-educated workforce raises productivity and
innovation, helping explain why some countries grow faster than others (see
Development Economics).

**Spillovers:** Education benefits society beyond the individual through ideas,
innovation, and healthier, more engaged citizens—justifying public investment.

## Limits and policy

**Financing constraints:** Credit-constrained families under-invest in education
even when returns are high, because they can’t borrow against future earnings—a
source of poverty traps.

**Match matters:** Formal schooling alone isn’t enough; skills must match
labor-market needs for returns to materialize.

**Policy role:** Public schooling, scholarships, and job-training programs
address under-investment and spread the gains of human capital more broadly.
