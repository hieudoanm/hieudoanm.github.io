## What is it?

The **Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)**, associated with Eugene Fama, holds
that asset prices fully reflect all available information. If true, it is
impossible to consistently beat the market by trading on that information—prices
already incorporate it. The EMH is the intellectual foundation of passive,
index-based investing.

## Three forms

**Weak form:** Prices reflect all past price data. Technical analysis—predicting
future prices from past patterns—cannot systematically generate excess returns.

**Semi-strong form:** Prices reflect all publicly available information
(financials, news). Fundamental analysis of public data cannot consistently beat
the market, because prices adjust almost instantly to new public disclosures.

**Strong form:** Prices reflect all information, including private and insider
information. No one—even insiders—can reliably beat the market.

## Evidence and challenges

**Supporting:** The overwhelming majority of actively managed funds fail to beat
low-cost index funds over long periods— consistent with prices already being
efficient.

**Anomalies:** Seasonal effects, value and momentum premiums, and post-earnings
drift suggest systematic patterns inconsistent with full efficiency.

**Behavioral finance:** Psychological biases (see Overconfidence Bias, Mental
Accounting) lead to mispricing that efficient markets would rule out.

## The practical lesson

**Indexing works:** Even if markets aren’t perfectly efficient, they are hard to
beat after costs—so diversified, low-cost index funds beat most active managers.

**Insider trading is illegal:** The strong form’s most extreme claim is
precisely why insider trading is prohibited— information advantage would let
insiders profit unfairly.

**A lively debate:** The EMH is one of finance’s most contested ideas, dividing
believers in market efficiency from behavioral economists who see persistent,
exploitable mistakes (see Behavioral Finance).
