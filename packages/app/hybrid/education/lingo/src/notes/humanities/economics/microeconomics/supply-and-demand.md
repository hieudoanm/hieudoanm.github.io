## What is it?

**Supply and demand** is the foundational model of market prices. **Demand**
captures how much of a good buyers want at each price (typically more at lower
prices), while **supply** captures how much sellers are willing to provide
(typically more at higher prices). The price where the two meet is the
**equilibrium**—where the quantity buyers want exactly matches what sellers
offer.

## The demand side

**The law of demand:** As price rises, quantity demanded falls. Higher prices
make a good less attractive and pull spending toward substitutes.

**Determinants beyond price:** Income, tastes, the price of substitutes and
complements, and expectations shift the whole demand curve—moving it left or
right rather than along it.

**The demand curve:** Slopes downward. A movement along it reflects a price
change; a shift reflects a change in an underlying determinant.

## The supply side

**The law of supply:** As price rises, quantity supplied rises. Higher prices
make production more profitable, drawing in more sellers and more output.

**Determinants beyond price:** Input costs, technology, expectations, and the
number of sellers shift the supply curve.

**The supply curve:** Slopes upward. Costlier production at the margin means
sellers need higher prices to supply more.

## Why it matters

**Market equilibrium:** At the crossing point, there is no shortage or surplus.
Prices naturally gravitate there: excess supply forces prices down, excess
demand pushes them up.

**The invisible hand:** Adam Smith observed that individuals seeking only their
own gain are guided to produce what society wants, as if by an invisible
hand—supply and demand is its mechanism.

**Policy tools:** Price ceilings, floors, taxes, and subsidies all distort the
market by moving prices away from equilibrium, producing shortages or surpluses.

**Allocation:** Prices ration scarce goods to those who value them most and
signal producers where to direct resources.
