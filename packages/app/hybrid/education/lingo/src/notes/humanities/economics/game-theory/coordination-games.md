## What is it?

A **coordination game** is one where players benefit most from matching the same
action, rather than from beating rivals. Unlike a Prisoner’s Dilemma, there is
little conflict of interest—everyone prefers to coordinate. The problem is
choosing _which_ of several equally fine outcomes to land on. Driving on the
left or right, adopting a standard, and using a common language are all
coordination games.

## Multiple equilibria

**Shared interest:** In a pure coordination game, players prefer to do the same
thing. Both driving-on-the-right and driving-on-the-left are orderly; mixing
them is the disaster.

**Which one wins is arbitrary:** Multiple equilibria can exist, and which is
selected may be arbitrary—history, habit, or a focal point determines it, not
intrinsic superiority.

**Pareto-ranked options:** Sometimes one coordinated outcome is better for
everyone than another. Players then face a shared desire to select the superior
equilibrium, but may be trapped in a worse one.

## Focal points and the stag hunt

**Focal points (Schelling points):** Thomas Schelling showed that people
coordinate on salient, obvious outcomes even without communication—a landmark
time and place stands out. Salience breaks the tie between equilibria.

**The stag hunt:** A coordination game where two hunters can together catch a
stag (big reward) but risk failing, while each can safely catch a hare alone.
Everyone prefers the stag, but risk-aversion can trap them in the safe-but-worse
hare equilibrium.

**Trust is central:** Unlike the Prisoner’s Dilemma, the stag hunt rewards
mutual trust—the problem is coordinating on the risky but better outcome.

## Real-world applications

**Standards:** Keyboard layouts (QWERTY), video formats, and measurement systems
are coordination outcomes—everyone benefits from matching the standard, even if
another might be marginally better.

**Currency and language:** A common currency and a shared language have value
precisely because everyone uses them.

**Technology adoption:** Network effects make platform choices coordination
games—everyone joins the platform others are on.

**Social conventions:** Norms, etiquette, and traffic rules are self-enforcing
coordination outcomes with no one in charge.
