## What it measures

MEG sensors detect the magnetic field generated by the same intracellular
postsynaptic currents that produce scalp EEG, but from a different component of
the current geometry. Cerebral blood flow, skull movement, and other slow
sources contribute negligibly, so the signal is **essentially pure
neurophysiology** rather than a mixture requiring as much physiological noise
modelling.

The amplitude is the whole problem. Typical brain fields are **10–500
femtotesla** — roughly a million times smaller than the Earth's magnetic field
and near the noise floor of the room itself. A shielded room, a degaussing
system, and cryogenic hardware are not optional extras; they are what makes the
measurement possible at all.

## Why the skull does not blur it

This is MEG's central advantage. Electrical potentials are _volume-conducted_:
they smear as they pass through low-conductivity skull and high-conductivity
CSF, which is why scalp EEG has no principled inverse solution. Magnetic fields
are affected by **magnetic permeability, not conductivity**, and bone, CSF, and
brain are all nearly identical to air in that respect.

The consequence: the field measured outside the head is a faithful sum of the
sources inside, with far less spatial distortion. Combined with the fact that a
dipole field decays as the inverse _cube_ of distance, MEG is markedly more
sensitive to **deep** sources than EEG — thalamic and hippocampal generators are
detectable in MEG but heavily attenuated at the scalp.

## Sensors and instrumentation

Conventional systems use **SQUID** (superconducting quantum interference device)
flux transformers, cooled to around 4 K in a liquid-helium cryostat. Their
weakness is geometry: a helmet-sized dewar of liquid helium must be refilled
regularly, and the subject must keep still, because the sensors cannot follow
the head.

The reason SQUID works so well is **gradiometry**. Each sensor measures the
_difference_ in field between two points ~4 cm apart, so uniform ambient field —
the Earth, the building, the building's elevators — cancels exactly. One
reference channel is also measured inside the shield to model and subtract
whatever common-mode field remains.

Modern systems run 300+ channels, sample above **1 kHz**, and restrict analysis
to a usable band of roughly 0.01–200 Hz. That band is not a hardware limit but a
noise limit: ambient field and thermal noise rise sharply at low frequency.

## Reference-free reconstruction

Because whole-head coverage makes an intact-helmet assumption possible, MEG can
be reconstructed **without choosing a reference at all**. This removes the
arbitrary montage decision that complicates scalp EEG, though the cost is a
distributed, spatially smeared point-spread function whose extent grows with
depth — a trade, not a free win.

The practical families: **beamforming** (LCMV, DICS) works well for
well-separated sources but is biased when sources overlap; **beamformers
constrained by MRI** (sLORETA, eLORETA, MNE) improve spatial precision; and
**beamforming with parametric empirical Bayes** is the current accuracy leader
for evoked responses. Combining MEG with scalp EEG, which has complementary
sensitivity to different source orientations, is standard in modern pipelines.

## Strengths and limits

- **Direct, single-neuron temporal resolution.** The only non-invasive method
  resolving sub-10 ms dynamics, which is what makes it the instrument of choice
  for latency-ordered processing chains.
- **Less spatial blurring than EEG,** and genuine depth sensitivity because of
  the inverse-cube falloff.
- **Minimal physiological noise,** so artefact rejection is less interpretive
  than in EEG.
- **Enormous cost and fixed geometry.** Multi-million dollar installations, a
  shielded room the size of a small studio, periodic helium refills, and
  participants who must hold still because the sensors cannot follow a moving
  head. MEG is a laboratory instrument, not a clinical or wearable one.
