## Question 1 — Project contributions (≤150 words total)

**Purpose:** Concisely document your own role in the project organization. The rubric rewards clear, informative organization; contributions need not appear in every category.

- **Defining the question** (suggested 20–35 words)
  - What task did you personally perform: propose a question, refine its scope, connect it to a feasible dataset, operationalize variables, or clarify a hypothesis?
  - What decision or discussion did your contribution influence?
  - If the question changed, identify your role without claiming sole responsibility for a group decision.
- **Finding, exploring, and preparing data** (suggested 35–45 words)
  - Identify your own role in locating/accessing the data and understanding its source, sample, variables, units, and collection context.
  - Note concrete exploration/preparation you performed: checking structure and types, missing values, duplicates, ranges, coding, transformations, or inclusion/exclusion decisions.
  - Mention a decision only if you participated in it and can accurately explain why it was made.
- **Visualization** (suggested 25–35 words)
  - Name the type or purpose of visuals you personally made or explored (e.g. distributions, group comparisons, relationships, missingness, or exploratory plots); do not imply a particular chart was used unless it was.
  - Say briefly what the visualization helped you inspect or communicate. Include exploratory figures even if they were omitted from the final group report.
  - If visual design or uncertainty is relevant, Franconeri et al. (2021) provide a research review on communicating patterns, and Sacha et al. (2016) discuss uncertainty in visual analytics.
- **Analysis** (suggested 25–35 words)
  - State which analysis, coding, checking, or interpretation tasks you completed and your level of responsibility.
  - Distinguish running a procedure from selecting, understanding, checking, or interpreting it.
  - Mention teamwork accurately; avoid listing analysis steps you did not perform.
- **Editing strategy:** Use first person (“I”) to make individual contribution clear. Combine related tasks and remove general project background so this section remains within its shared 150-word cap.

## Question 2 — Dataset strengths, weaknesses, and learning (≤200 words)

**Question focus:** How well did the dataset address your defined question, and what did you learn about the data while working with it?

- **Anchor the evaluation to the question** (roughly 20–30 words)
  - Restate the question briefly and name what kind of data would be needed to address it.
  - State whether the dataset measures the relevant concepts, population, time period, or conditions.
- **Evaluate strengths** (roughly 40–50 words)
  - Identify specific properties that make the data useful: relevant variables, suitable observations or coverage, repeated measurements, useful variation, adequate resolution, or accessible structure—only where true for your dataset.
  - Explain how each property helps answer the question; avoid calling a dataset “large,” “representative,” or “high quality” without evidence.
- **Evaluate weaknesses** (roughly 50–60 words)
  - Select the most consequential observed issues: sampling or representativeness, limited sample size, missingness, measurement validity, uneven groups, coarse temporal/spatial resolution, confounding, inconsistent formats, or restricted metadata.
  - Explain how a limitation constrains a comparison, estimate, generalization, or causal conclusion. Do not list every possible issue.
- **Reflect on exploration and preparation** (roughly 40–50 words)
  - Describe a concrete thing you discovered during exploration or preparation that changed, complicated, or sharpened your understanding of the question.
  - Explain what prompted the insight (a plot, data check, variable definition, source documentation, or preparation decision) and what you learned from the process.
  - Where data handling is relevant, Broman and Woo (2018) discuss spreadsheet organization; Wilson et al. (2017) give practices for documented data workflows.
- **Synthesis** (roughly 15–25 words)
  - State the dataset’s overall fitness for this particular question, including the most important qualification.
  - For alternative data-processing choices or robustness concerns, Steegen et al. (2016) is a candidate source; cite it only if it directly fits the project.
- **Critical standard:** Tie every evaluation to a concrete property of your data and its consequence. The rubric's highest band asks for critical insight and specific learning, not generic strengths/weaknesses.

## Question 3 — Results, conclusions, and extensions (≤200 words)

**Question focus:** What matters most in your results, do they answer your question, how strong are the conclusions, and what would improve understanding?

- **Identify the main result** (roughly 35–45 words)
  - Select the result most relevant to the original question, not every output produced.
  - Describe its direction/pattern and uncertainty accurately; use the project’s values/figures if needed, but do not copy unverified numbers into the reflection.
- **Judge whether the question was answered** (roughly 35–45 words)
  - State whether the result answers the question fully, partly, or not yet, and explain the basis for that judgment.
  - Distinguish what the analysis shows from a stronger causal, mechanistic, or population-level claim it cannot establish.
- **Critically evaluate the conclusion** (roughly 35–45 words)
  - Identify the key limitation that most affects interpretation (for example, measurement, sampling, missing data, model/analysis assumptions, uncertainty, or robustness to decisions)—only if applicable.
  - Consider whether conclusions change under reasonable alternatives where this was examined or could feasibly be examined. Steegen et al. (2016) discuss transparency about reasonable data-processing choices; Munafò et al. (2017) discuss transparent and reproducible research practices.
- **Propose an improvement or extension** (roughly 45–55 words)
  - Specify what data or analytic change you would make: a better measure, broader or more balanced sample, additional observations, relevant covariates, longitudinal or experimental data, a robustness check, a justified alternative visualization, or a targeted follow-up analysis.
  - Explain the information it would provide and how it would address the limitation. Avoid a vague statement such as “collect more data” without saying which data and why.
  - If visual communication is central, Franconeri et al. (2021) or Panagiotidou and Vande Moere (2022) may support discussion of visualization and uncertainty, respectively.
- **Closing sentence** (roughly 15–20 words)
  - Give a restrained conclusion: what the project contributes and what evidence would be needed to reach a firmer conclusion.
- **Critical standard:** The rubric rewards clear conclusions and a specific extension that would improve understanding. Make the proposed next step feasible and connect it directly to the question and observed result.

## Rubric alignment checklist

- **Project organization:** Are your individual contributions concise, informative, and easy to follow?
- **Dataset insight:** Have you evaluated actual dataset properties in relation to the question, with a clear explanation of both strengths and limitations?
- **Learning reflection:** Do you identify a specific thing learned through exploration and explain how it changed or refined your understanding?
- **Conclusions:** Do you identify the most important result, assess whether it answers the question, and calibrate claims to the evidence?
- **Extensions:** Is the next step specific, feasible, and clearly linked to a limitation or unanswered part of the question?
- **Language:** Is the reflection logically organized, clear, efficient, and specific rather than generic?
