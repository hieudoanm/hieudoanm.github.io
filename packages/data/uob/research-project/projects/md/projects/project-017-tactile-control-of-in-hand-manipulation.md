## Project title

Tactile control of in-hand manipulation

## Project summary

This MSc project will investigate the sensorimotor mechanisms underlying voluntary object rotation within a precision grip. The task will model a familiar everyday action such as picking up a pencil from a table with surface normal grip force generated by the thumb and index finger coordinated with lift (tangential load) force from the arm and then rotating it into a preferred writing orientation, using force generated by the middle finger to create a destabilising torque that is controlled in conjunction with the normal grip force. Participants will perform a series of controlled object-rotation movements while thumb – index finger forces, middle finger generated torques, and object kinematics (rotation) are recorded to examine how the nervous system coordinates manipulation and stability during dexterous hand actions.

The project builds on the torque stabilisation framework developed by Wing and Lederman (1998), which demonstrated how the digits coordinate force and torque production to maintain stable object orientation during precision grip. A central aim will be to distinguish the contributions of feedforward and feedback control by manipulating both frictional conditions (dry versus wet fingertips) and the quality of somatosensory input (bare fingers, single surgical gloves, and double surgical gloves). These manipulations are expected to alter both the frictional coupling between the digits and the object and the availability of tactile feedback, thereby changing the balance between anticipatory control and online correction.

The work is further motivated by recent evidence that sensory impairment compromises dexterous manipulation, including findings from Roberts et al. (2025) demonstrating deficits in precision grip control in individuals with chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN). By identifying how sensory and mechanical factors influence rotational stability during within-hand manipulation, the project will advance understanding of skilled hand function and provide a foundation for future investigations of dexterity impairments associated with sensory loss, ageing, and neurological conditions. More broadly, the study may provide a quantitative and mechanistically grounded complement to in-hand manipulation assessment tools such as those reviewed by Pont et al (2009) and being developed by Liu and colleagues (2018, 2025), helping to link clinical measures of manual dexterity with the underlying sensorimotor processes that support skilled object manipulation.
