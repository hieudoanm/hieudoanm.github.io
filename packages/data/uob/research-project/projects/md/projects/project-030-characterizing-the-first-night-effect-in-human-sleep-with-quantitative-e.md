## Project title

Characterizing the First Night Effect in Human Sleep with Quantitative EEG (qEEG) Markers

## Project summary

This project focuses on the first night effect, a well-recognized phenomenon in sleep research in which a person’s first night in a sleep laboratory differs from subsequent nights because of the unfamiliar environment, monitoring equipment, or anticipatory stress. These adaptation-related changes can affect both subjective sleep experience and objective sleep measures, potentially influencing the interpretation of laboratory sleep recordings.

The study will examine the first night effect using quantitative EEG (qEEG) markers, allowing a more detailed assessment of brain activity than standard sleep-stage scoring alone. By looking more closely at EEG dynamics, the project aims to identify subtle changes in cortical arousal, sleep stability, spectral power, and other neurophysiological features that may not be captured by conventional analyses.

The project will have the opportunity to explore these effects across the full sleep laboratory experience, including the pre-sleep period, during sleep, and post-sleep during the morning wake period. There is also potential to further explore individual differences in the first night effect including age, personality traits, chronotype or baseline sleep characteristics.

## Methodology

This is a pre-existing dataset.   The student will have access to data from healthy participants who have completed at least two overnight sleep laboratory recordings, enabling comparison between the initial laboratory night and a subsequent night. Polysomnographic data have been collected throughout the stay in addition to next day alertness and vigilance testing. Detailed quantitative EEG (qEEG) analyses will be used to assess changes in brain activity beyond standard sleep-stage scoring (which is already available). qEEG markers can be examined during the pre-sleep, intra-sleep, and post-sleep periods to capture anticipatory arousal, sleep microstructure, and any residual morning effects. This markers may include aperiodic signals, graph metrics, local sleep and other qEEG markers. Measures of individual differences, including trait anxiety, habitual sleep quality, and age, are available as predictors of response.
