## Project title

Dissociating neural pathways for selective attention

## Project summary

Selective attention enables us to focus on goal-relevant information in the environment, while discarding irrelevant distraction. It is thought to rely upon top-down signals that are generated in the prefrontal cortex and amplify processing of goal-relevant information in sensory cortex. While this general framework is widely appreciated, the mechanisms by which attention biases perception are poorly understood.

Recent neuroimaging studies suggest that different forms of attention may utilise distinct neural pathways that connect unique sub-regions of the prefrontal cortex and the visual system: spatial attention may rely upon top-down signals from the frontal eye-field (FEF) that the dorsal visual stream, whereas non-spatial attention may rely upon top-down signals from the inferior frontal junction (IFJ) that target the ventral visual stream. This project seeks to put this hypothesis to a rigorous causal test using transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) as a tool to transiently perturb these putative pathways.

## Topic

selective attention, prefrontal cortex, brain stimulation, psychophysics

## Methodology

Participants perform computerised tasks requiring spatial and non-spatial forms of selective attention, while FEF, IFJ, and a control region are stimulated in different sessions using a theta-burst protocol that is known to transiently reduce neuronal excitability. Examining the disruptive effects of TMS as a function of task demand and targeted brain region will provide unprecedented insights into the functional neuroanatomy of selective attention.

Overall, the project’s findings will significantly enhance our understanding of attention and its impact on neuropsychiatric conditions. Additionally, it may generate compelling evidence that the classic distinction between dorsal and ventral visual streams extends into the human prefrontal cortex.

Working on the project will entail reading relevant literature, collecting behavioural data, delivering TMS (after receiving training), and contributing to the analysis, interpretation, and presentation of results.
