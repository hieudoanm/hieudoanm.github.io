## Project title

Reshaping behavioural Interventions for Sleep in NeuroGenetic syndromes (RISING)

## Project summary

The prevalence of poor sleep is elevated in children with an intellectual disability compared to age-matched peers without neurodevelopmental conditions (Quine, 2001; Richdale, Francis, Gavidia-Payne, & Cotton, 2000). In children with an intellectual disability, estimates vary between 16-84%, with settling and waking problems being particularly common (Johnson, 1996; Quine, 1991; Wiggs & Stores, 1996). Co-occurring conditions that can exacerbate poor sleep, include: secondary health conditions resulting in pain (e.g. constipation, gastro-oesophageal reflux, respiratory tract infections, otitis media, dental caries), epilepsy (e.g. fragmented sleep architecture, abnormal EEG activity, the presence of nocturnal seizures, or the prescription of anti-epileptic medication), and co-occurring psychiatric diagnoses (e.g. anxiety, depression, sedatory side-effects of benzodiazepines). In addition, there are several neurogenetic syndrome groups where there are phenotypic physiological characteristics that are known to exacerbate poor sleep. For example, in Smith-Magenis syndrome loss of function or mutation to the retinoic acid-induced 1 gene (RAI1) affects circadian rhythms (DeLeersnyder et al., 2001; Potocki et al., 2000) resulting in an inverted melatonin release pattern and distinct sleep profile characterised by early morning waking and excessive daytime sleepiness. Physical facial features of Down syndrome confer risk for obstructive sleep apnoea (Lee et al.,2018; Trois et al., 2009), resulting in a distinct sleep profile of sleep disordered breathing.

First-line behavioural interventions focus on the promotion of good sleep hygiene practices (e.g. limited access to electronic devices before bed, a sleep-conducive environment and bedtime routine; Jan et al., 2008) and parent education to support a behavioural intervention approach, such as graduated extinction (Malow et al., 2012; Rigney et al., 2018). However, research into the efficacy of individualised targeted behavioural interventions (e.g. graduated extinction) for children with neurogenetic syndromes is virtually non-existent despite evident effectiveness in autism, ADHD and intellectual disability populations (Rigney et al., 2018). A move towards intervention is particularly important, given sleep problems in children with developmental delay predict the frequency and severity of daytime behaviours that challenge (e.g. aggression, overactivity; Adams, Matson, & Jang, 2014; Didden et al., 2002) and given the association between poor sleep and negative cognitive outcomes/executive functioning deficits in some neurogenetic syndrome groups (e.g. Joyce et al., 2020).

The main aim of the current study is to examine sleep and daytime outcomes in children with neurogenetic syndromes receiving tailored behavioural sleep interventions. Using subjective and objective measures of sleep (e.g. sleep questionnaires, sleep diaries, actigraphy) and daytime outcomes (e.g. behavioural questionnaires, executive functioning tasks), we will investigate changes in sleep following intervention and associations between sleep parameters and daytime outcomes. Parents/caregivers of children aged 4-15 years who meet eligibility criteria will be invited
to participate. Participants will complete baseline assessments prior to implementing a tailored sleep intervention. Following the intervention period, the same assessment battery will be re-administered to allow pre-post comparisons of child and main caregiver sleep, child daytime functioning outcomes and parent/caregiver wellbeing. In addition, qualitative interviews will be conducted with approximately 5-8 parents/caregivers following their involvement in the intervention, to explore perceptions of intervention acceptability and feasibility.
