## Project title

Thinking, Believing, and Deciding: Cognitive and Personality Predictors of Misinformation Susceptibility

## Project summary

The proliferation of misinformation — false or misleading content presented as factual — has been identified as a significant public health and social concern, with documented consequences for vaccination uptake, political behaviour, and intergroup relations (Lewandowsky et al., 2017; Pennycook & Rand, 2021). Understanding who is susceptible to misinformation, and why, has therefore become a major priority for cognitive, social, and personality psychology. Despite a rapidly growing empirical literature, key theoretical questions remain unresolved. Does analytical thinking ability offer genuine protection against false belief, or does the motivation to think analytically matter just as much? Do personality traits predict misinformation susceptibility directly, or is their influence channelled through self-regulatory mechanisms such as metacognition and self-control? Remarkably, few studies have tested these questions within an integrative framework that considers cognitive ability, cognitive style, personality, and epistemic beliefs simultaneously. The overarching aim of this project is to examine the cognitive and personality predictors of susceptibility to misinformation, with particular attention to the mechanisms and boundary conditions through which these individual differences operate.

The project uses existing data from two large-scale online surveys collected in 2021 and 2022, with over 900 participants in each dataset prior to cleaning (approximately 500–600 usable responses per dataset following exclusions). Data were collected internationally, resulting in heterogeneous samples spanning multiple nationalities, educational backgrounds, and occupations. The datasets include validated measures of analytical thinking ability (Cognitive Reflection Test), cognitive motivation (Need for Cognition), critical thinking dispositions, metacognitive awareness, self-control, Big Five personality traits, conspiracist beliefs, and beliefs about the role of cognition in emotion regulation, as well as two measures of fake news belief and endorsement spanning health, social, political, and environmental misinformation topics. Demographic information is also available, including age, gender, educational level, occupation, nationality, and daily internet use.

This project will suit students who are interested in cognitive psychology, social psychology, and the psychology of belief. It is particularly relevant to those who wish to develop advanced quantitative data analysis skills, including moderation and mediation analysis using regression-based methods (e.g., Hayes' PROCESS macro). The ability to conduct and interpret these analyses is increasingly valued by employers and is especially important for those considering further academic research, including doctoral study. Students will receive guidance and support from the project supervisor as they develop these skills, and are expected to engage critically with the psychometric properties of the measures, the cross-sectional nature of the data, and the implications of a global convenience sample for the reliability and generalisability of their findings.
