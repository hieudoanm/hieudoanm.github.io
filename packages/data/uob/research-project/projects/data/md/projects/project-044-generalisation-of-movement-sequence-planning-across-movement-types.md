## Project title

Generalisation of movement sequence planning across movement types

## Project summary

Sequential movements such as handwriting and typing are prominently present in everyday life. These movements typically involve a set of separate movement elements that the brain needs to execute in quick succession. It remains unclear how the central nervous system can plan and perform such movement elements so seemingly effortlessly. To address this, there is a longstanding debate in neuromotor control on what the brain considers as a movement. Does the brain break down a movement into separate elements or does the brain essentially fuse these different elements into a single ‘holistic’ action? Fusing the different movement elements into a single holistic movement would require the brain to chunk the elements into a new neural representation.
Once sequential movements become overtrained (and can be retrieved from memory), the brain may start treating multiple movement elements as one. This is known as fusion and can happen at the behavioural and neural level. Whilst it is relatively well established that humans can fuse movement elements behaviourally, less is known about neural fusion. In fact, most research suggests that there is an absence of neural fusion. This is mostly backed up by primate literature. In a landmark study in 2021, Zimnik and Churchland showed that primates who had to perform single and double reaching movements, neuronal activity of the brain's motor areas is not different for both movements, suggesting that neural fusion does not happen. One criticism of this paper is that the two reaching movements were very similar, meaning that the absence of neural fusion could be the result of behavioural transfer. Instead, one should therefore pick two sequential movements in which this should not occur by essentially maximising the behavioural differences between the two movements.
The current MSc project will comprise the collection and analysis of electro-encephalographic (EEG) data. The analysis will particularly focus on how similar these two movements are prepared by the brain. This will involve analysing the EEG data using multivariate pattern analysis (e.g., competitive queueing [Kornysheva et al., 2019] and representational similarity analysis).
