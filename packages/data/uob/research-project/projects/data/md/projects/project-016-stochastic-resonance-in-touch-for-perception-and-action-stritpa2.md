## Project title

Stochastic resonance in touch for perception and action (STRITPA2)

## Project summary

Sensations from the hands and feet are fundamental to the way we perceive and interact with our environment. Touch receptors transmit afferent information to the brain via neural activity in peripheral nerves and the spinal cord, providing critical information about physical contact. When this transmission is disrupted, by aging or through disorders such as peripheral neuropathy, both perception and control of action are affected. Basic sensory experiences such as tactile detection and discrimination in the hand and foot can be impaired, and actions such as grasping and maintaining balance compromised.
Recent studies have consistently shown that adding random vibration (“noise”) at sub-threshold levels can enhance the detection and transmission of weak tactile signals (through a process sometimes referred to as “stochastic resonance”), and thus improve balance and hand function1. For example, sub-threshold vibration delivered at the wrist has been shown to benefit fingertip tactile detection2, suggesting the potential utility of a vibrating wrist band for stroke rehabilitation3. Sub-threshold wrist vibration has also been shown to benefit individuals with chemotherapy induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN)4. Random noise at 60% detection thresholds presented at the wrist improved detection of tactile events at the fingertip compared with when wrist vibration was absent or presented at 120% detection thresholds. Moreover, finger movements driving hand aperture during reaching exhibited reduced normalized jerk (see Fig1), suggesting improved kinesthesis (combining proprioception and tactile sensation).
Vibration at the wrist is likely to be transmitted to and excite mechanoreceptors throughout the hand5. This activation is believed to underlie improvements observed in somatosensory function with sub-threshold vibration. The proposed project aims to:
(i) Extend the approach of Osumi et al4, testing the effects of wrist vibration in healthy older adults or people with chemotherapy induced peripheral neuropathy (due to cancer treatment) across a range of behavioural measures of passive and active fingertip touch function;
(ii) Computationally examine the origins of wrist vibration benefits by simulating vibration effects on the population of touch afferents in the hand. This will be accomplished using TouchSim6, developed by Saal et aal and previously successfully used by Wing et al to account for age-based decline in 2-point discrimination threshold7.
